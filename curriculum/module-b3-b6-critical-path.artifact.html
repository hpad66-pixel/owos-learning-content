B3

What a Gantt chart actually shows

A Gantt chart is a picture of your schedule. Each bar is a task. How far left it starts tells you when it begins; the length tells you how long it takes. Bars that line up are happening at the same time.

A Gantt maps activities against time. The power isn't the bars — it's the dependencies: the logic that says this waits on that. A bar's position is driven by its predecessors, not chosen by hand.

A Gantt is a communication tool and a control baseline. Once you set a baseline, every status conversation becomes planned-vs-actual instead of opinion. Insist the logic is real — a Gantt with no dependencies is a decorated to-do list.

The four dependency types

Most links are Finish-to-Start (FS): the second can't start until the first finishes. The others: Start-to-Start, Finish-to-Finish, and the rare Start-to-Finish. A lag adds waiting; a lead lets tasks overlap.

B6

The critical path — the chain that sets your finish date

Some tasks have float — slack, room to slip. Others have zero. String every zero-float task together, start to finish, and that chain is your critical path. It's the longest path through the job, so it sets the finish date.

Think of a relay race with several lanes running at once. The team finishes only when the slowest lane crosses the line. That slowest lane is the critical path. On Millpond it's the valve order → install → test → tie-in → acceptance. The permits finish early and then wait.

You find it with two passes. The forward pass sets each task's earliest start/finish. The backward pass sets the latest each can happen. Where the two are equal, float is zero — that task is critical. The Studio ran both passes live as you built it.

The critical path is your attention budget — where your money, best people, and risk mitigation belong. Watch for near-critical paths: they become critical the moment one thing slips, and they're where projects get blindsided.

The water-job truth

On most utility jobs the critical path is not the construction — crews are fast. It's the gates in front of it: the permit that takes weeks to condition, the valve on a 26-week lead, the outage window. That's why the permits sat with float while procurement ran critical. Manage the gates, not the digging.

“On track” is the most dangerous phrase in project management — because most of the time nobody has checked which chain the date actually hangs on.

When you must go faster

Two moves, and only on the critical path (compressing anything else is wasted money): crashing — throw resources at a critical task; and fast-tracking — overlap tasks. Try the Crash install −3 chip and watch the finish move.

B4

Predecessors, successors & lag

Every dependency has two ends. The task you wait on is the predecessor; the task waiting is the successor. Sometimes there's built-in waiting between them — concrete curing, a mandatory hold, a lab turnaround. That's lag, and it sits on the link, not inside a task.

Open the Dependencies tab in the Studio: every task's predecessors and successors are laid out, and you can add lag to any link and watch the finish move. On Millpond, add a 2-week bac-t lab lag before tie-ins and you'll see the in-service date slide — because that link is on the critical path.

why it matters

Lag is where hidden weeks hide. "Disinfect, then tie in" looks instant on a bar chart — but the two passing bac-t samples take a lab turnaround. Put that on the link as lag and the schedule tells the truth instead of a hopeful lie.

C-load

Cost loading — the schedule becomes a cash-flow forecast

Cost loading assigns each activity its dollars and spreads them across its weeks. Add it all up week by week and you get a cash-flow forecast; the running total draws the S-curve. Open the Cost-loaded tab to see Millpond's.

It answers a simple question your finance team always asks: how much will we spend, and when? The tall bars are your heavy-spend weeks — on Millpond, when the valve order lands and installation runs.

The cost-loaded schedule is the backbone of your SRF/grant draw schedule and your monthly pay-estimate forecast. It's also the baseline for earned value later: planned value is the S-curve.

A believable S-curve is a financing tool. It tells the funder when you'll draw, tells the board when cash leaves, and turns "we'll spend about $1.3M" into a dated forecast you can be held to.

R-load

Resource loading & leveling

Resource loading stacks each task's crews week by week into a histogram. When demand crosses your capacity line you're over-allocated — the plan is impossible, not just tight. Open the Resource-loaded tab: install needs 3 crews against a capacity of 2, so those weeks show red.

The fix is resource leveling: move tasks that have float until demand fits, or add a crew, or extend. Leveling on tasks with float costs you nothing; leveling on the critical path pushes the finish. That's why float (from the Schedule tab) and resource loading are read together.

the trap this catches

A bar chart can show ten crews' worth of work all "on schedule" in the same week — and it's a fantasy if you have three crews. The histogram is the reality check: it turns "on schedule" into "actually staffable."

Check yourself

1. A task has 4 weeks of float. It slips 2 weeks. Does the project finish late?
Show answer
No. It had 4 weeks of slack; using 2 leaves 2. The finish doesn't move — but it's now near-critical, so watch it.
2. In the Studio, drop the valve order to 8 weeks. Why does the critical path jump to the permits chain?
Show answer
Once the valve is fast, the longest chain to the finish is no longer through procurement — it's permits → mobilize → install → test → tie-in → accept. The critical path is always the longest path, so it moves.
3. You need to save 3 weeks. Your critical path is procurement-driven. Does adding a second install crew help?
Show answer
No — install isn't controlling the date, procurement is. Crashing a non-critical task spends money for zero schedule gain. Expedite the valve instead.
4. The Resource-loaded tab shows install needs 3 crews but capacity is 2. Name two ways to fix it.
Show answer
(1) Level — if any concurrent work has float, shift it out of those weeks so demand drops to 2. (2) Add capacity — bring a third crew (cost) or extend install so fewer crews work at once (schedule). If install is on the critical path, leveling it pushes the finish — so you'd weigh the extra crew against the delay.
5. You add a 3-week lab lag before tie-ins on the Dependencies tab and the finish slips 3 weeks. What does that tell you?
Show answer
That link is on the critical path — the lag has zero float to absorb it, so it pushes the in-service date one-for-one. Lag on a critical link is as dangerous as a long task.
The takeaway
  • The critical path is the longest chain of dependent tasks — it sets your finish date.
  • Float is slack; zero float means critical. Protect the zero-float chain.
  • Predecessor / successor / lag are the links; lag sits on the link and is where hidden weeks hide.
  • Cost-load the schedule and it becomes a cash-flow S-curve — your draw schedule and EVM baseline.
  • Resource-load it and the histogram shows over-allocation; leveling uses float to make it staffable.
  • On water jobs the critical path is usually permits and long-leads, not the digging — and it moves.
