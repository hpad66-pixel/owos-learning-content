# Staff Direction for M58: Stormwater, CSO/SSO and Flood/Overflow Prediction

## Your assignment

Teach stormwater, combined-sewer overflow, sanitary-sewer overflow, flood, and overflow prediction through rainfall, antecedent conditions, assets, telemetry, models, field evidence, alerts, and public consequence.

Open with this situation: A forecast shows higher-than-normal overflow risk, but radar uncertainty, blocked inlets, sensor gaps, tide, soil condition, and field reports may change the response.

Ask the learner to decide before teaching begins: **Determine what an alert means, what uncertainty remains, and which person or agency decides the response.**

Use this analogy once as the front door, then return to the utility work: A weather warning creates time to prepare. It does not guarantee that every street will flood or decide which gate to close.

The learner must leave with a saved **storm and overflow prediction response plan**. If the module does not produce that
record and evidence, the module is not complete.

## Non-negotiable learner outcomes

1. Explain Stormwater, CSO/SSO and Flood/Overflow Prediction in plain English and connect it to a real water, wastewater, stormwater, or One Water decision.
2. Distinguish forecast, nowcast, alert, exceedance, flood, combined-sewer overflow, sanitary-sewer overflow, verification, and response without using one term to hide several different jobs.
3. Analyze the module situation, identify missing evidence, and name the professional consequence of a weak decision.
4. Compare how the same question changes across drinking water, wastewater, stormwater, administration, engineering, finance, technology, and public leadership.
5. Evaluate sources, assumptions, limitations, permissions, and human authority using this boundary: Do not present prediction as certainty or automate public warning and operating actions without approved authority, thresholds, communication, and incident procedures.
6. Create a usable storm and overflow prediction response plan with evidence, roles, limits, review steps, and the next accountable action.
7. Defend the recommendation to Foundation, Practitioner, and Leader audiences while keeping disagreement and uncertainty visible.

authority for load-bearing water, public-sector, privacy, cybersecurity, accessibility, regulatory,
or legal claims. Give exact page, section, table, figure, or paragraph locators.

- What must an intelligent newcomer understand about Stormwater, CSO/SSO and Flood/Overflow Prediction before using it in utility work?
- Which sources can support the distinctions among forecast, nowcast, alert, exceedance, flood, combined-sewer overflow, sanitary-sewer overflow, verification, and response?
- What evidence would allow a practitioner to make this decision: Determine what an alert means, what uncertainty remains, and which person or agency decides the response.
- Which failure modes, counterexamples, and edge conditions could make the storm and overflow prediction response plan misleading?
- How does the question change across drinking water, wastewater, stormwater, reuse, finance, engineering, administration, technology, and public accountability?
- Which actions require qualified operator, engineer, water-quality, cybersecurity, privacy, legal, procurement, records, finance, accessibility, or executive review?
- Which claims remain outside the module boundary: Do not present prediction as certainty or automate public warning and operating actions without approved authority, thresholds, communication, and incident procedures.

## Curriculum and learning-design team

Use backward design from the **storm and overflow prediction response plan** and the completion evidence. Align each
outcome to teaching, practice, feedback, assessment, and a saved record. Keep Foundation,
Practitioner, and Leader views connected to the same completion standard. The module belongs in
**AI Across the Water Lifecycle** and must not absorb the learning job of an adjacent module.

Reject vague outcomes such as know, learn, appreciate, or become familiar with. Require the learner
to explain, distinguish, analyze, evaluate, create, or defend something visible.

## Writing team

Write to one intelligent utility professional who is new to the topic. Define every term before
using it. Explain the major ideas already present in the source sequence:

- `Learning objectives`
- `The problem`
- `Prediction`
- `Compliance link`
- `Knowledge check`
- `Role takeaways`
- `Glossary`
- `Next`
- `Sources`

These headings are source inventory, not permission to keep weak prose. Every section must serve an
outcome, the opening decision, the work product, or the evidence boundary. Use the module situation
and one strong analogy. Do not invent a utility, incident, quotation, statistic, law, standard, or
source.

## Assessment team

Use this varied sequence as the starting design: true or false with correction, ordering, evidence classification, final applied check. Place each check beside
the idea it evaluates. Give immediate explanatory feedback and retry. The final applied check must
inspect required fields in the **storm and overflow prediction response plan**. Scrolling, time spent, confidence, and an
unreviewed reflection do not prove completion.

## Articulate and production team

Build only from the approved manuscript and import package. Preserve module, section, proposal,

## Marketing and enrollment team

You may say that the module helps the learner **determine what an alert means, what uncertainty remains, and which person or agency decides the response** and produce a
**storm and overflow prediction response plan**. You may not promise savings, compliance, certification, safety,
implementation success, career advancement, or professional mastery. All public claims require a
separate source and approval record.

## Quality-control team

Reject the module when:

- the learner cannot explain the core distinctions among forecast, nowcast, alert, exceedance, flood, combined-sewer overflow, sanitary-sewer overflow, verification, and response;
- the opening situation disappears and the module becomes a glossary or product tour;
- the work product has no source, role, limitation, review, or next-action fields;
- a current section, proposal, subtopic, enhancement, or contributor record is omitted or moved
  without provenance;
- a visual has no teaching conclusion or an interaction has no consequence;
- assessment does not test the stated outcomes or provide feedback and retry;
- a role example changes the standard instead of the context;
- marketing makes an unsupported outcome claim;
- accessibility and phone use are left for later; or
- the evidence, learning-design, practitioner, technical, or release gate remains unresolved.

## Evidence and scope boundary

Do not present prediction as certainty or automate public warning and operating actions without approved authority, thresholds, communication, and incident procedures.
