# One Water AI Learning Pathways

One foundation. Five ways to apply it. One accountable pilot.

Every participant completes the same One Water AI journey, then applies it through the lens of the decisions they influence.

**Authority boundary:** One shared curriculum, one universal core, and five role lenses. The lenses change application, examples, coaching, and portfolio evidence. They do not duplicate or approve curriculum.

## One Water AI Core

**Understand the whole system before you change one part of it.**

Build a common language for AI, data, governance, water operations, economics, human authority, and implementation so every profession can make better decisions together.

**Audience:** Everyone who influences water, wastewater, stormwater, technology, finance, governance, public policy, products, or organizational change.

**Problem:** AI programs fail when specialists use different language, work from disconnected evidence, or optimize one function without seeing the consequence elsewhere in the system.

### Demonstrated objectives

- Explain AI, retrieval, workflows, agents, and knowledge graphs in plain language.
- Connect an AI idea to water service, evidence, affected people, decision rights, and consequence.
- Choose the least complicated architecture that can perform a bounded task.
- Evaluate data readiness, provenance, permissions, failure, cost, and human review.
- Build and defend a governed 90-day pilot with measurable stop and success conditions.

### Outcomes

- A shared One Water AI decision vocabulary
- A data and knowledge readiness map
- A governed opportunity portfolio
- A tested prototype evidence package
- A defended 90-day pilot and one-year roadmap

### Portfolio evidence

- One Water AI Fieldbook
- source-backed implementation portfolio
- governed AI application record
- capstone defense

### Water-sector examples

- A drinking-water team distinguishes advisory support from treatment authority.
- A wastewater team connects permit evidence, maintenance history, and shift decisions.
- A stormwater team combines maps, inspections, rainfall, and public reports without hiding uncertainty.

**Analogy:** Think of the core as the legend on a shared system map. Every profession sees a different part of the network, but the legend lets them interpret the same evidence together.

**Call to action:** Explore the shared journey

## Leadership and Public Value

**Lead the system, not the demonstration.**

Connect public purpose, service, evidence, risk, investment, workforce, and human authority into decisions the organization and community can understand.

**Audience:** General managers, executive directors, C-suite leaders, elected officials, board members, public administrators, and cross-functional One Water leaders.

**Problem:** Leaders are asked to approve AI before the organization can explain the problem, evidence, operating consequence, full cost, or accountable owner.

### Demonstrated objectives

- Ask the questions that reveal hidden assumptions, unsupported claims, and unclear authority.
- Compare opportunities by public value, readiness, consequence, affordability, and trust.
- Define an operating model with named decision rights and escalation.
- Explain a staged AI roadmap to technical, operational, financial, and public audiences.

### Outcomes

- executive decision field card
- public governance review
- AI operating-model canvas
- one-year organizational roadmap

### Portfolio evidence

- board-ready decision brief
- portfolio sequence
- governed pilot charter

### Water-sector examples

- A board challenges an AI purchase using a public-value and consequence record.
- A utility executive sequences data, workforce, security, and pilot investment instead of funding a stand-alone demo.

**Analogy:** The leader is the conductor, not every instrumentalist. The work is to keep specialists in time, preserve their authority, and make the complete performance accountable.

**Call to action:** Build your leadership roadmap

**Source audience profiles:** c-suite, elected-public-leader, one-water-cross-functional

**Module view:** 15 governed Fellowship modules

## Operations and Technical Delivery

**Keep the physical system at the center of the digital decision.**

Use AI as bounded support for operations, maintenance, engineering, planning, and delivery while qualified people remain accountable for the system.

**Audience:** Operators, maintenance professionals, engineers, asset managers, capital-delivery teams, planners, laboratory teams, and utility technical leaders.

**Problem:** Digital tools become unsafe or useless when they are detached from field conditions, operating procedure, asset history, professional judgment, and the official record.

### Demonstrated objectives

- Connect field observations, records, models, requirements, and decision history.
- Design support workflows that preserve operator and professional authority.
- Test usefulness and failure against realistic water, wastewater, and stormwater cases.
- Create an evidence-backed technical pilot with review and stop conditions.

### Outcomes

- operator-support boundary card
- engineering evidence chain
- One Water relationship map
- tested technical pilot

### Portfolio evidence

- asset or process workflow
- failure and escalation log
- architecture and control decision

### Water-sector examples

- An operator retrieves the approved procedure and exception history but remains the decision maker.
- An engineer traces a requirement from planning through design change, field condition, cost, and approval.

**Analogy:** A clean digital model is a drawing. Operations is the pressure test. The system is only credible when it survives contact with the field.

**Call to action:** Design your technical application

**Source audience profiles:** operator-maintenance, engineer-capital-delivery, planner-strategist

**Module view:** 19 governed Fellowship modules

## Finance, Administration, and Governance

**Make every dollar, record, approval, and consequence traceable.**

Connect records, cost, procurement, risk, privacy, approvals, and public accountability so useful AI can move without losing control of the decision.

**Audience:** Finance executives, budget professionals, administrators, clerks, records teams, procurement, legal, privacy, risk, audit, and assurance professionals.

**Problem:** The apparent price of AI often excludes data repair, integration, review, security, change, failure, records, and exit cost.

### Demonstrated objectives

- Trace the official record, source, approval, and retention boundary.
- Build a complete cost and value baseline rather than a model-price estimate.
- Evaluate vendors, permissions, privacy, risk, and segregation of duties.
- Create a defensible investment, procurement, and assurance record.

### Outcomes

- records and approval map
- AI total-cost model
- procurement scorecard
- governed application record

### Portfolio evidence

- lineage trace
- financial decision record
- control and assurance plan

### Water-sector examples

- A clerk distinguishes a helpful draft from the official record and the authority to release it.
- A finance team prices the complete operating system, including review, security, integration, change, and exit.

**Analogy:** The governance record is the chain of custody for a decision. If the handoffs disappear, the answer has no more standing than an unlabeled sample bottle.

**Call to action:** Build your defensible decision record

**Source audience profiles:** administrator, clerk-records, finance-executive, budget-capital-finance, data-governance-assurance

**Module view:** 19 governed Fellowship modules

## Data, Technology, and AI

**Connect the technology decision to the utility consequence.**

Design architecture, context, identity, tools, evaluation, security, and orchestration around the real work and authority of a One Water organization.

**Audience:** Chief AI Officers, CIOs, CISOs, data leaders, enterprise architects, developers, security teams, data stewards, and technical product owners.

**Problem:** A technically impressive system can still fail because its sources, permissions, evaluation, operating owner, cost, or stop conditions are missing.

### Demonstrated objectives

- Choose among chat, retrieval, workflow, single-agent, and multi-agent patterns.
- Design trusted context with metadata, lineage, provenance, and permission.
- Define identity, least privilege, evaluation, incident, and escalation controls.
- Build and defend the smallest useful architecture for a real utility task.

### Outcomes

- data readiness map
- enterprise AI architecture record
- identity and permission matrix
- evaluation and incident plan

### Portfolio evidence

- source-grounded answer test
- tool-use evidence log
- governed AI operating model

### Water-sector examples

- A Chief AI Officer selects retrieval instead of an agent because the task needs evidence, not autonomy.
- A security lead limits a tool to approved read-only records and verifies every request and result.

**Analogy:** Connecting a tool is like issuing a badge. The badge does not decide the job, the allowed rooms, the supervisor, or when access must stop.

**Call to action:** Design your governed AI architecture

**Source audience profiles:** cio-ciso-caio, data-governance-assurance

**Module view:** 31 governed Fellowship modules

## Builders, Advisors, and Solution Partners

**Build the thing, then prove it belongs in the real system.**

Move from idea to a useful, testable, procurement-ready pilot without skipping the user, operating context, evidence, security, economics, or authority.

**Audience:** Product builders, entrepreneurs, vendors, consultants, advisors, integrators, researchers, and people who want to create something useful for the water sector.

**Problem:** Water-sector ideas often begin with technology and reach the user, evidence burden, integration boundary, procurement path, and operating consequence too late.

### Demonstrated objectives

- Frame a measurable professional problem before choosing technology.
- Build the smallest useful prototype and expose its failure modes.
- Translate evidence, architecture, governance, cost, and adoption for a utility buyer.
- Create a procurement-ready proof package and governed pilot proposal.

### Outcomes

- product problem brief
- utility value and evidence record
- working prototype evidence package
- procurement-ready pilot

### Portfolio evidence

- tested prototype
- failure and correction log
- 90-day product pilot

### Water-sector examples

- A builder replaces a generic chatbot demo with a source-grounded workflow and visible human decision point.
- A vendor presents test evidence, integration limits, full cost, and exit terms before making an outcome claim.

**Analogy:** A prototype is a pressure test, not a showroom model. The work matters when it holds under real data, real users, real failure, and real accountability.

**Call to action:** Turn your idea into a governed pilot

## Common questions

### Do I need to know how to program?

No. You need a real work context, professional judgment, and the willingness to test an idea against evidence.

### Is this one course for every role?

It is one shared curriculum with different application lenses. Everyone learns the same system, then applies it to the decisions and evidence of their role.

### Does the program authorize operational or professional decisions?

No. The program develops judgment and work products. Qualified people and organizational authorities retain responsibility for consequential decisions.

### Are outcomes, pricing, and enrollment open to the public now?

No. These pages are governed internal previews until claims, proof, terms, privacy, enrollment, and publication are approved.
