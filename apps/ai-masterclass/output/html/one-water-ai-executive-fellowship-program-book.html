The position

Professional judgment, not content access

The water sector does not need another general course that explains what artificial intelligence is. It needs leaders who can tell the difference between a demonstration and a defensible utility application. It needs operators, engineers, managers, data leaders, procurement teams, consultants, and executives who can work from the same language, test the same evidence, and identify the person who remains accountable.

The One Water AI Executive Fellowship is a six-month applied program for professionals responsible for water, wastewater, and stormwater decisions. Participants learn the technology in plain English, apply it to utility work, build with current tools, test failure and governance controls, and leave with an implementation portfolio for one real organizational opportunity.

This is not a collection of videos. It is a working program. Every course produces part of the participant's One Water AI Fieldbook. Every studio converts an idea into a decision, diagram, control, or work product. Every claim must carry its source and limitation. Every proposed use case must name its data, knowledge, risk, owner, measurement, stop condition, and next action.

What participants leave able to do

  1. explain machine learning, generative AI, retrieval, knowledge graphs, skills, tools, agents, and agentic systems without hiding behind jargon;
  2. distinguish chat, retrieval, deterministic workflow, single-agent, and multi-agent approaches;
  3. diagnose whether a utility problem is ready for AI or still requires data, process, knowledge, governance, or organizational repair;
  4. build and test prompts, reusable Claude Skills, source-grounded retrieval patterns, and bounded agent workflows;
  5. evaluate output quality, provenance, permissions, failure modes, cost, and human-review effort;
  6. identify valuable applications across operations, maintenance, water quality, customer service, capital delivery, regulation, finance, and administration;
  7. create an AI governance and procurement record suitable for organizational review;
  8. compare value, feasibility, consequence, adoption cost, and implementation risk;
  9. lead cross-functional discussion between utility practice, data, technology, legal, security, procurement, and executive leadership; and
  10. define and evaluate goals, environments, tools, memory, multi-agent handoffs, human authority, and stop conditions;
  11. trace an AI application through development, release, monitoring, incident response, and retirement;
  12. recognize synthetic or manipulated content and protect the provenance of public and internal records; and
  13. defend a governed 90-day pilot and a one-year organizational roadmap.

  2. intended outcome, affected people, and measurable value;

The companion system

The Fieldbook keeps the 64 modules connected

The One Water AI Fieldbook is part workbook, part laboratory notebook, part decision record, and part executive playbook. Every module adds one decision, map, test, reflection, or professional record. By Module 64, those records form the participant's implementation portfolio.

Participants retain their private entries. Confidential organizational material is not shared unless the participant chooses to submit it for review.

Repeating module rhythm

  1. Before: What do I currently believe, and what decision would I make?
  2. Learn: What relationship, mechanism, or distinction matters?
  3. Apply: What does this mean for one utility task or organizational situation?
  4. Keep: What record enters my implementation portfolio?

From research to instruction

Every module follows the same governed production path

Research tools help find material. They do not become the evidence. The original source, exact locator, reviewer, and limitation stay with the claim.

1

Define value

Name the utility decision, audience, work product, and professional value.

2

Discover sources

Use Perplexity or another research tool to find source leads, then open the originals.

3

Preserve evidence

Register direct URLs, files, dates, permissions, and exact locators.

4

Verify claims

Check every material claim against the original source and record limitations.

5

Write the manuscript

Produce one complete Markdown teaching paper in plain English.

6

Design graphics

Describe visuals that explain a process, relationship, comparison, or decision.

7

Assemble learning

Build the approved lesson in Articulate with interactions, checks, and accessibility.

8

Review and release

Run factual, practitioner, learning, accessibility, and publishing gates.

The controlled Markdown file

One module record carries the research, teaching, and visual direction

What the manuscript contains

  • the module value proposition and audience;
  • research questions, source inventory, and claim ledger;
  • a complete instructor explanation in plain English;
  • a utility example, limitation, failure, and decision method;
  • graphic instructions that state what each visual teaches;
  • distributed assessments with explanatory feedback;
  • frequently asked questions, citations, and approval state.

Graphics must teach

A graphic is chosen because a process, relationship, comparison, cause, hierarchy, quantity, place, or change is easier to understand visually. Decorative technology images do not count.

Each visual states what it must show, how the learner uses it, the conclusion the learner should reach, its accessible description, and its mobile behavior.
