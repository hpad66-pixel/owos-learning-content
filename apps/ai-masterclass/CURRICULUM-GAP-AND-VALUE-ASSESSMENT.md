# Curriculum Gap and Value Assessment

## Executive finding

The original eight-module syllabus is a strong foundation course on agents. It is not broad enough
to support a six-month, $10,000 executive fellowship by itself.

The revised program preserves that course as one part of a larger sequence. It adds technical
fluency, data and knowledge readiness, Claude Skills, tool and connector design, agentic systems,
utility applications, governance, cybersecurity, procurement, economics, organizational change,
hands-on studios, and a defensible capstone.

## What the original syllabus did well

- It distinguished a chatbot, retrieval-supported system, workflow, agent, and multi-agent system.
- It explained the complete agent loop and agent anatomy.
- It treated agenticness as a degree of delegated control rather than a marketing label.
- It included orchestration, handoffs, shared state, streaming events, guardrails, and human authority.
- It connected concepts to water, wastewater, and stormwater situations.
- It produced a Utility Agent Canvas and a 90-Day Pilot Brief.
- It made failure literacy, evidence, cost, and the simplest-solution decision part of the course.

That content remains valuable as Course 3 of the fellowship.

## What MIT's public curriculum makes clear

MIT's current executive AI programs combine several learning jobs: technical foundations, machine
learning and data, problem formulation, natural language processing, multimodal AI, strategy,
the human element, governance, workshops, and a capstone. MIT AI Essentials also uses an editable
participant playbook, hands-on bot and agent building, team simulation, and an adoption roadmap.

The lesson for OWOS is not to reproduce MIT. The lesson is that a premium executive program needs
technical fluency, organizational judgment, practice, peer learning, a tangible playbook, and an
applied conclusion. Content alone does not earn the price.

## Distinctive OWOS advantage

MIT carries institutional prestige, broad faculty depth, and an established executive credential.
OWOS should not pretend to possess those advantages today.

OWOS can offer something materially different:

- instruction written specifically for water, wastewater, and stormwater work;
- a systems view that connects natural assets, infrastructure, people, data, knowledge, decisions,
  and public consequences;
- a governed knowledge graph beside the learning experience;
- source-level provenance and correction rather than citations added after writing;
- utility practitioners, technology specialists, regulators, consultants, and vendors in the same
  decision room;
- reusable work products tied to actual utility implementation;
- a participant-owned fieldbook that becomes an organizational portfolio; and
- continuing access to a One Water learning and expert community.

## Remaining gaps before the fellowship may be sold at $10,000

The curriculum is now broad enough as a master blueprint. The program itself is not yet ready for
sale. The following work remains:

1. identify and contract credible instructors, reviewers, and utility practitioners;
2. secure permission and evidence for all required sources and case material;
3. build and test all 64 Articulate modules;
4. define live forum and studio facilitation standards;
5. build the complete fieldbook and its digital privacy model;
6. recruit a small design cohort before enrolling 100 participants;
7. establish assessment, identity, attendance, and credential controls;
8. validate workload with working executives;
9. define technical support, accessibility, refund, substitution, and cohort policies;
10. build employer sponsorship materials and a measurable organizational value report;
11. complete LearnWorlds, Articulate, OWOS, graph, community, and learning-record integration; and
12. obtain independent curriculum, practitioner, accessibility, security, and legal review.

## Recommended first release sequence

1. Build Course 1 and its eight Fieldbook records.
2. Use Module 1 as the golden lesson.
3. Build and test the Claude Skill studio because it is a visible differentiator.
4. Pilot Course 1 with 10 to 15 invited professionals.
5. Revise the teaching and fieldwork from observed evidence.
6. Build Courses 2 and 3, then run a three-course design cohort.
7. Complete the remaining five courses before opening the full fellowship.

This sequence protects the $10,000 position. The first 100 participants should encounter a program
that has already survived learner use, practitioner challenge, and implementation review.
