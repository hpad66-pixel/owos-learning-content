# One Water AI Executive Fellowship

## Commercial and learning proposition

The fellowship is a 24-week, 64-module applied core for professionals who influence water,
wastewater, and stormwater decisions. Its target tuition is $10,000 per participant. The price is
supported by guided instruction, live executive forums, applied studios, expert and peer review, a
participant-owned Fieldbook, a source-backed implementation portfolio, and a capstone defense.

Two optional extension lines deepen the program without making every participant complete work that
does not fit their role. The 48-hour Builder Bridge develops the practical foundations needed to
build, test, connect, and release small systems. The 48-hour Advanced Agent Systems line develops
formal design judgment for agent planning, retrieval, memory, multi-agent coordination, human-agent
interaction, continual learning, and water robotics. A readiness diagnostic determines whether a
participant should take either line.

The program is not sold as access to content. It is sold as the development of professional
judgment and a governed path from one organizational problem to a defensible 90-day pilot.

## Defensible competitive position

One Water AI is designed around a combination that the reviewed comparison programs do not present
as one integrated curriculum: One Water domain practice, data and knowledge governance, applied AI,
agent systems, human authority, production controls, organizational adoption, and a governed pilot
portfolio. That is a defensible point of difference. It is not permission to claim that no other
program anywhere contains any similar topic. All market language must identify the comparison set,
the review date, and the features being compared.

## Working purpose

Help water, wastewater, and stormwater professionals understand AI clearly enough to ask better
questions, recognize sound and unsound uses, participate in governed decisions, and design bounded
utility applications that keep people accountable.

## Learners

- utility executives and managers
- operations, maintenance, engineering, water quality, and customer-service professionals
- data, technology, cybersecurity, legal, procurement, and governance professionals
- consultants, vendors, regulators, researchers, and nonprofit collaborators working with utilities

## Series rule

The series will be a family of courses, not one oversized course. Each course has a single promise,
its own audience and evidence boundary, a clear connection to the One Water knowledge graph, and a
usable work product.

## First course

**Working title:** AI Fluency for One Water

**First module:** AI and utility work: what changes, what does not, and who remains accountable.

This first module package is a controlled authoring example. Its factual claims remain pending
source review. It is not ready for public instruction or Articulate publishing.

## Non-negotiable outcomes

Every module must enable a learner to:

1. explain the concept in plain English;
2. connect it to a real utility decision, record, asset, or role;
3. distinguish evidence, interpretation, scenario, and opinion;
4. identify where a person must remain accountable; and
5. create or improve a work product they can use after the lesson.

Across the program, participants must also be able to:

1. choose among a fixed workflow, retrieval, a single agent, and a multi-agent system;
2. define goals, environments, tools, memory, permissions, stop conditions, and human authority;
3. test usefulness, factual support, resilience, cost, drift, and failure recovery;
4. trace an AI application from development through release, monitoring, incident response, and
   retirement;
5. recognize manipulated or synthetic content and preserve content provenance;
6. translate adoption risk into participation, training, process redesign, and decision rights;
7. measure value against a real baseline rather than a demonstration; and
8. defend what remains unknown, unsafe, unapproved, or outside the evidence.

## Program evidence boundary

The curriculum blueprint governs what will be taught. It does not by itself prove instructional
quality, market superiority, learner achievement, credential validity, or production readiness.
Those claims require completed lessons, verified authorities, assessment evidence, accessibility
review, delivery testing, instructor preparation, and formal release approval.
