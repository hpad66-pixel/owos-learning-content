A consequential utility situation

The deadline is tomorrow. The records disagree.

Harbor County must prepare a stormwater evidence packet for a pending review. Inspection records, geographic information, maintenance work, rainfall data, photographs, and prior correspondence do not tell the same story.

Instructor explanation

This opening decision asks you to choose an operating structure before you know which answer the course prefers. A single general agent may sound simple, but it can hide who checked the records and who had authority. A fixed workflow can be predictable, but it may not know what to do when two records disagree.

Read the three choices as if you were assigning real utility work. Ask which option preserves the evidence, makes the conflict visible, and leaves the final decision with an accountable person. Your choice will not change the facts. It changes how the work is controlled.

What the team knows

Thirty-two outfalls appear in one system. Twenty-nine appear in the inspection log. Two photographs have no asset identifier. A maintenance record says one structure was cleaned, while the field note says access was blocked.

This is an instructional scenario. It is designed to teach coordination and evidence control. It is not a regulatory conclusion.

Your first call

How should the work be organized when several records must be compared, every material statement needs support, and a named person must approve the final packet?

Before you read the funnel
The funnel does not mean that most records are useless. It shows that a record needs more than relevant words before it can support a consequential statement. A photograph without an asset identifier may be informative, but a reviewer cannot safely use it to prove the condition of a specific outfall.
Read from top to bottom. At each level, ask why a record was removed or held back. The final twelve statements are not twelve guesses. Each one must point to an exact record, the correct asset, the applicable date and version, and a person who can accept or return the conclusion.

  Broken-handoff laboratory
  
Break the handoff. Then make it defensible.

  
A handoff fails when the next role receives work without the context, evidence, identity, or authority needed to continue safely. Pick a failure and repair it.

  
How to use the laboratory
A broken handoff can look like a small clerical mistake at first. The problem grows when later roles treat missing context as if it were confirmed. This laboratory lets you introduce one failure safely and see which parts of the downstream work become uncertain.
Choose a failure on the left. Read the effect, then inspect the status grid from context through decision. Select the repair that fixes the original cause, not merely the final wording. Run the repaired handoff and compare the result. In utility work, a useful repair preserves the source and clarifies authority before the packet reaches the approver.

  Permission ladder and human authority
  
Capability does not equal permission.

  
An agent may be technically able to change a record. That does not mean it is allowed to. Set the least authority each role needs for this lesson scenario.

  
How to set authority
The five levels move from opening information to triggering an external action. Each step creates a larger consequence. Read and recommend are usually easier to reverse. Write and execute can change a governed record or affect the world outside the lesson.
For each role, choose the lowest level that lets it complete its assigned job. Do not grant authority because the technology can use it. The evidence roles need access to records, the drafting role needs permission to prepare a packet, and the human approver keeps the authority to accept, reject, or return consequential work.

  Professional work product
  
Build the contract before you build the agents.

  
The handoff contract records what each role receives, what it must prove, what it may do, and when a person must take over.

  
Why this work product matters
The contract turns an attractive demonstration into a reviewable operating design. It names the person who owns the outcome, the information that may travel between roles, the proof required at each boundary, and the conditions that stop the system.
Write the fields as instructions another utility employee could test. Avoid phrases such as use good data or escalate when needed. Name the required locator, the conflict that causes a stop, the person who receives the escalation, and the actions the system may never take. The live preview becomes a draft you can review with operations, data, legal, security, and program staff.

  Deterministic knowledge checks
  
Can you defend the handoff?

  
What these checks measure
These checks test operating judgment, not memorized vocabulary. You will identify what must cross a handoff, place the control steps in order, respond to a record conflict, and explain why limitations belong in the approval packet.
Use the feedback as instruction. A wrong answer does not reset the lesson. It shows which control is missing and lets you try again. You have mastered this part when you can explain the reason for the sequence in your own words.

Source map and instructional boundary
What supports this lesson.
This lesson uses public guidance on agent evaluation, identity, authorization, least privilege, and operational-technology risk. Primary references include National Institute of Standards and Technology agent standards work, National Institute of Standards and Technology identity and authority guidance, Open Worldwide Application Security Project guidance on excessive agency, Anthropic guidance on agent evaluations, and United States Environmental Protection Agency operational-technology guidance.
Harbor County and its records are instructional. The lesson does not authorize autonomous operational control, regulatory certification, emergency action, procurement approval, or financial authorization. A qualified utility professional must review any real application.
