Part C | Domain D12 | Assurance review

“We have a policy” is a claim, not proof

Riverbend's governance report says critical datasets are owned, access is reviewed, quality issues are resolved, and suppliers delete data at exit. The assurance team asks for evidence. The room goes quiet. You will convert those statements into testable claims and an improvement cycle.

PROOF REQUIRED
Audit committee request

Can you report that access review is effective?

The policy requires quarterly review. A spreadsheet contains manager initials, but it does not show the reviewed population, exceptions, removals, dates, system coverage, or independent verification.

1 | Break a claim into testable parts

Evidence must be good enough for the sentence you want to say

Control claim is the sentence under review. “All privileged utility-data access is reviewed quarterly and inappropriate access is removed promptly” is testable because it names a population, activity, frequency, and intended result. “We take security seriously” is not.

Assurance evidence can include system configuration, logs, tickets, approvals, samples, observations, interviews, reconciliation, monitoring results, and retained outputs. Evidence quality depends on the claim. A screenshot may prove that a setting was visible at one moment. It may not prove that the setting covered every account for the entire quarter.

Worked access-review test

A signed sheet becomes a bounded conclusion

Reviewer Elena first reconciles the access-review export to the identity platform and finds that two laboratory systems were omitted. She defines the full in-scope population as 214 privileged accounts across nine systems. The quarterly workflow has evidence for 196 accounts. Twelve contractor accounts were approved without current sponsors. Six accounts belong to retired service identities that cannot yet be removed because of a legacy integration.

Elena does not write “the control failed” and walk away. She concludes: “The quarterly review operated for 196 of 214 in-scope accounts. Coverage was incomplete for two laboratory systems. Twelve sponsorship exceptions and six legacy service-account exceptions remain. Management accepted temporary controls for the service identities through September 30; the contractor accounts are suspended pending sponsor confirmation.” The conclusion is narrower, more useful, and reproducible.

2 | Operate assurance as a loop

Testing is not the end of the story

Control testing can examine design, implementation, and operating effectiveness. A well-written procedure may have good design but no evidence of use. A completed activity may operate but address the wrong population. A control may work today while new systems, suppliers, data uses, or risks make its design obsolete.

Read this cycle as one operating discipline. Begin with a precise claim and risk. Gather evidence from the actual population, not a convenient folder. Test against stated criteria. Record the conclusion and limitation. Correct the cause, verify the result, and use what you learned to improve the control and the next test.

1. ClaimWhat must be true?
2. EvidenceWhat would show it?
3. TestHow will we evaluate?
4. RespondWho corrects what?
5. LearnWhat changes next?

Do not stop at a finding. Follow it through cause, action, verification, residual risk, and updated control design.

Continuous improvement is evidence changing the way governance operates, not a growing list of unresolved actions.

3 | Review evidence without overclaiming

Open three evidence files

Finding should identify criteria, condition, cause, consequence, and affected scope. A corrective action is more than closing a ticket. It should change the control, evidence, training, system, ownership, or process that allowed the condition.

Use the evidence file like a skeptical but fair reviewer. Select each item and compare what it proves with what the control claim says. The review does not reward the largest document. It rewards relevance, traceability, coverage, reliability, and a clear limitation. Notice how the conclusion changes as evidence strength changes.

Ask exactly which part of the claim each artifact supports and which part remains unknown.

Evidence can support existence, performance, or effectiveness. Do not silently upgrade one kind of proof into another.

Evidence ranking check

Which conclusion can the reviewer defend?

Select the conclusion whose confidence matches the available evidence. Strong assurance language requires complete scope, suitable criteria, reliable evidence, reproducible testing, and resolved or visible exceptions. If the evidence is incomplete, the honest answer is a bounded conclusion, not false certainty.

Questions from assurance reviews

Evidence and improvement FAQ

Is a screenshot reliable evidence?

It can support a narrow point at one time if the source, date, identity, and context are known. It rarely proves complete operation across a period or population by itself.

Must assurance be independent?

The required independence depends on consequence and purpose. Self-monitoring is useful, but material claims may need a reviewer with sufficient objectivity, competence, authority, and access to evidence.

What is the difference between an issue and a finding?

An issue is a condition needing attention. A formal finding connects observed condition to criteria, scope, evidence, cause, consequence, ownership, response, and verification expectations.

Can we sample instead of testing everything?

Often, if the sampling approach fits the claim and risk. Document the population, method, rationale, exceptions, confidence, and what the sample cannot establish.

When is a corrective action complete?

When the authorized owner implements it, evidence shows the intended change, residual risk is addressed, and effectiveness is verified after enough operating time.

How does assurance support continuous improvement?

Patterns in exceptions, incidents, overrides, tests, complaints, and outcomes should change priorities, control design, training, automation, metrics, and future assurance plans.

Evidence boundary. The access-review facts are instructional and do not constitute an audit opinion, certification, regulatory conclusion, or control assurance. Define applicable criteria and reviewer independence for the utility and jurisdiction. Reference points include the U.S. Government Accountability Office Yellow Book, the Institute of Internal Auditors standards resources, and the NIST Cybersecurity Framework.
