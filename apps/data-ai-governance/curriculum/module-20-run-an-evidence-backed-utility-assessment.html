A maturity number without evidence is decoration

Riverbend's leadership deck says data governance maturity is 3.7 out of 5. No one can explain which criteria were used, who was interviewed, what evidence was sampled, how confidence was calculated, or why two plants with different practices received the same score. You will run an assessment that can survive questions.

Open the fieldbook
Working assessment | no certification or benchmark claim
Executive briefing in ten minutes

Do you present the 3.7 score?

The number came from a workshop vote. Participants used different definitions of “managed,” several domains had no operational representation, and no documents or system records were tested.

Field note 1 | Define the assessment

Start with the decision the assessment must support

An evidence-backed assessment is not a survey with a score attached. It answers a decision such as: Which governance capabilities must Riverbend strengthen before expanding AI-assisted operations? Which domains create immediate regulatory, safety, customer, financial, or service risk? Which improvements are feasible in the next budget cycle?

Write the scope before collecting evidence: organizations, business units, plants, systems, data domains, use cases, time period, criteria, and exclusions. Define the assessment criterion in observable language. “Ownership is mature” is vague. “Each critical data product has an accountable owner who approves definition, use, access, quality thresholds, and material change, with retained evidence” can be tested.

Do not let the score become the product. The useful outputs are evidence, findings, uncertainty, dependencies, priorities, and agreed next actions. A number is only a compact view of that reasoning.
Field note 2 | Follow a reproducible sequence

Six steps keep the assessment honest

Read the fieldwork roadmap as a chain of custody for the assessment conclusion. Each milestone creates evidence for the next. If scope is vague, sampling becomes arbitrary. If criteria are vague, interviews become opinion. If evidence is not traced, scoring becomes persuasion. Move through the milestones in order, preserve every limitation, and make the next action traceable to the finding.

1. FrameDecision, scope, criteria
2. PlanPeople, sources, sample
3. GatherInterview, observe, inspect
4. TriangulateCompare claim and evidence
5. ConcludeScore, confidence, limit
6. ActFinding, owner, roadmap

A credible conclusion can be traced backward from action to finding, score, evidence, criterion, and scope.

Assessment quality comes from a reproducible evidence path, not the confidence of the facilitator.

Worked Riverbend domain

Assess metadata capability without guessing

The criterion says critical operational data elements must have approved definitions, owners, sources, units, valid-time rules, quality expectations, and consumer change notice. The assessment team selects twelve elements used in regulatory reporting, asset planning, and treatment optimization. Interviews suggest the catalog is “mostly complete.” Inspection shows that ten have descriptions, seven have owners, five have approved definitions, four state valid time, and only two retain evidence of consumer notice after change.

The team rates capability as “partially implemented” rather than converting the observed percentages into an invented decimal. Confidence is moderate because the sample covers three important decisions but excludes the customer domain and one laboratory platform. The finding is specific: Riverbend has catalog inventory but lacks decision-centered meaning, time, and change evidence for critical elements. The next action is not “buy a catalog.” It is to establish a governed profile and change workflow for the highest-consequence data products, then verify adoption.

Field note 3 | Separate score from confidence

A high score with weak evidence is still weak

Confidence is different from the capability score. A domain can appear strong while confidence is low because only one team was interviewed. Another can score low with high confidence because system evidence, observations, and records consistently show the gap.

Triangulation compares policy, interview, observation, system record, workflow evidence, sample, and outcome. Interviews explain practice and context. They should not be treated as proof that every required event occurred. Documents describe expected design. They do not prove operation. System evidence can show events but may omit the reason or quality of judgment.

Use this profile to resist a single-number story. The colored segments represent different evidence dimensions, not five maturity levels. Inspect scope, relevance, reliability, consistency, and limitation separately. Ask which source supports every segment and which evidence is missing. A balanced profile supports higher confidence. A missing dimension should remain visible instead of being averaged away or disguised by an attractive total score.

Report capability and confidence together so leaders can distinguish a known gap from an uncertain impression.

Field note 4 | Calibrate a conclusion

Build the score from evidence

Limitation is part of the result, not fine print. It can describe excluded systems, unavailable records, small samples, conflicting evidence, time pressure, or dependence on self-report. Limitation does not make an assessment useless. Hidden limitation does.

Choose the evidence conditions, then calibrate.
Confidence check

Which report line is defensible?

This check asks you to preserve both usefulness and uncertainty. The best report line identifies the observed capability, evidence scope, confidence, limitation, and consequence. It avoids unsupported decimals and avoids the opposite mistake of refusing to conclude anything at all.

Finding note

State criterion, observed condition, cause, consequence, scope, evidence, and confidence.

Questions from fieldwork

Utility assessment FAQ

Should every domain use the same maturity scale?

A common scale can help, but the criteria and evidence must remain domain-specific. Ownership, quality, privacy, OT, and AI capabilities do not look identical in operation.

How many interviews are enough?

There is no universal number. Select roles and sites that represent the scope, consequence, and variation, then combine interviews with documents, system evidence, observation, and samples.

Can a team score itself?

Self-assessment is useful for learning and preparation. Material funding, assurance, or public claims may require independent challenge, evidence testing, and transparent conflicts.

How do we compare plants fairly?

Use comparable scope, criteria, period, evidence, and operating context. Preserve legitimate differences instead of forcing every plant into the same implementation pattern.

What if evidence conflicts with interviews?

Do not average the conflict away. Trace definitions, time, scope, identity, and source reliability. Record the alternative interpretations and lower confidence until the conflict is resolved.

How often should the assessment be repeated?

Use a risk-based cadence and reassess after material changes, incidents, new systems, acquisitions, supplier changes, AI expansion, regulatory changes, or major corrective programs.

Evidence boundary. Riverbend's maturity score and findings are instructional. This lesson does not certify a utility, establish a regulatory rating, compare organizations, or authorize investment. Assessment criteria, independence, evidence, and scales require approved utility and jurisdictional governance. Reference points include the NIST Cybersecurity Framework, the U.S. Government Accountability Office Yellow Book, and the ISO 9001 quality management overview.
