What should the supervisor do first?

The crew needs a safe isolation plan now. The obsolete file should not remain in operational use, but deleting it may destroy evidence covered by the hold.

1 | Give each information object a lifecycle job

Current, historical, held, archived, and disposable are different states

A record is information retained as evidence of business or public-service activity. A document can be useful working material without yet being the official record, and a database row can be a record even if nobody printed it. Classification follows purpose, authority, and evidence, not file format.

Retention is how long a record must or may be kept under an authorized schedule. Disposition is the controlled final action. Supersession ends current use under the approved rule. Legal hold pauses disposition for records in scope. It does not make every copy current for operational use.

Separate usability from preservation. Riverbend can block a superseded plan from the crew’s current workspace while preserving the historical version and its metadata in the controlled record system.

Reconcile the valve plan without erasing history

The records manager identifies the approved plan series and its schedule. Engineering confirms revision 7 is current, approved, and effective. Operations removes revision 5 from the tablet sync set and verifies that revision 7 reaches every crew device. The records team preserves revision 5, its approval history, distribution evidence, and incident-related communications under the legal hold. This outcome avoids two common errors. Riverbend does not destroy evidence in the name of cleanup, and it does not leave obsolete instructions active in the name of preservation.

Debrief. The same file can be obsolete for field work, required for a hold, and later eligible for authorized destruction or archival transfer. Those states should be machine-readable where practical and understandable to staff.

3 | Classify the record series

Manage families of evidence, not random files

A retention schedule usually applies to a record series or class with a shared function and lifecycle, not to an arbitrary folder name. Riverbend links the valve-plan series to engineering design and configuration control. It separately classifies distribution acknowledgments, change approvals, incident communications, work orders, sensor events, and training records.

Read this tree from the service decision down to evidence families. Each branch may have a different owner, authority, sensitivity, retention trigger, and final disposition. The relationships let Riverbend retrieve a complete incident record without assigning one retention rule to everything. Notice that the approved plan, field execution, and incident response remain connected even when separate schedules govern them.

Good classification keeps related evidence connected while allowing each series to follow its authorized lifecycle.

4 | Make disposition a controlled process

Eligibility starts a review, not a silent delete

Before disposition, Riverbend confirms the schedule and exact trigger, checks for holds and investigations, identifies copies and systems, obtains authorized approval, performs the approved destruction or transfer method, reconciles expected and actual records, and retains a certificate or transfer record. For long-lived records, it also tests whether the format remains readable and whether identity, context, and integrity survive migration.

Step through the disposition gate. Each stage can stop the action. This protects against premature destruction, indefinite retention, orphaned supplier copies, and archives that exist but cannot be opened or understood. At the final Prove stage, look for the certificate, reconciliation, excluded records, transfer evidence, and schedule update that remain after the records themselves leave active systems.

Move through every gate. A failed hold or authority check stops the final action.

Disposition is defensible when Riverbend can show what was eligible, what was excluded, who approved, what happened, and what proof remains.

Questions from records operations

Lifecycle, retention, and records FAQ

Is every piece of data a record?

No. Record status depends on function, authority, and evidence requirements. Some data is transitory or duplicated, but a system value may still be a record when it supports a bill, permit, operation, right, decision, or obligation.

Can we keep everything forever?

Usually that creates cost, discovery, privacy, security, and usability risk. Follow authorized schedules and holds. Preserve what must be preserved, and dispose of eligible records through a controlled process.

Does a legal hold make a record permanent?

No. It suspends normal disposition while the hold applies. After authorized release, the record returns to the applicable schedule and any other duties or exceptions.

What about backups?

Document how schedules and holds apply to backups, recovery systems, snapshots, and immutable copies. A backup process is not automatically a records program, and restoration can reintroduce expired or held data.

Who approves destruction?

The authorized records process identifies the responsible owner and reviewer. Requirements vary by jurisdiction and record class. Individual users should not improvise final disposition.

How do we retain an artificial-intelligence decision?

Retain the decision and the evidence required to reconstruct it: purpose, user, context, source versions, prompt or configuration when applicable, model version, output, human review, action, limitations, outcome, and required rights or incident records.

Evidence boundary and release truth. The valve-plan facts are instructional. This lesson does not establish a legally valid schedule, hold scope, public-records response, archival decision, or disposition authority. Requirements vary by utility and jurisdiction. Use authorized records, legal, operational, privacy, security, and historical review. Anchors include the United States National Archives and Records Administration scheduling guide, the NARA scheduling records guidance, and the International Organization for Standardization 15489-1 records management page. D08 remains subject to records-professional, legal, practitioner, and source-owner review.
