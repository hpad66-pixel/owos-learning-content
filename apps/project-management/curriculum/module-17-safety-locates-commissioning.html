Chapter 17 · The work in the ground

Safety, locates, and commissioning

This is the chapter where the project stops being a plan and becomes a hole in the road with people in it. Three things decide whether that goes well: how you control hazards, what you know about what is already buried there, and how carefully you prove the new asset works before you hand it over.

The job, now in the ground

The Millpond Road water main replacement is under construction. An eight foot deep open trench runs down a residential street with a school on it. There is a gas main somewhere near the alignment, and in six weeks the new main has to be disinfected, cleared, and put into service without a boil water notice.

What you will be able to do

  • Apply the hierarchy of controls instead of reaching for personal protective equipment first
  • Run the one-call locate process and respect the tolerance zone
  • Coordinate the agencies who control the ground you are working in
  • Plan realistic commissioning time, and know what substantial completion really means

Health and safety, and the hierarchy of controls

Trenching is among the most dangerous work in our industry. A cubic yard of soil weighs about as much as a small car, and a collapse gives nobody time to move. The formal way to think about any hazard is the hierarchy of controls, and the reason it is a hierarchy rather than a list is that the controls are wildly unequal. Take the Millpond trench and try each one.

Read the residual numbers from the bottom up and the shape of the problem appears. Moving from personal protective equipment to a trench box takes far more risk out than anything you can do inside the bottom two rungs, and eliminating the trench takes out almost all of it. Yet safety programs spend most of their energy on the bottom two, because those are the ones you can enforce with a clipboard. Where the hazard is serious, spend the design decision, not the toolbox talk.

Use one test when you review a control: what happens when a person has a bad day? Directional drilling still removes the trench. A trench box still provides a physical barrier. A toolbox talk and a high-visibility vest depend on behavior and do little against collapse. That does not make lower controls useless. It means you should never present them as substitutes for eliminating or physically controlling a serious hazard.

Locates, right of way, and the agencies who own the ground

Before anyone breaks ground you have to know what is already down there, and you find out through the one-call system. In the United States you dial 811, and every utility owner in the area is notified to come out and mark their lines. Move through the sequence and notice that the ticket begins the control. It does not replace field verification at the conflict.

How to read it: move from notification through field verification and ticket maintenance. Each step adds evidence required for safe excavation.

Conclusion: a valid ticket and visible marks still leave location uncertainty that must be controlled in the field.

The financial case for doing this properly is not subtle. A utility strike on a gas main means an evacuation, an emergency response, a repair you pay for, a regulatory report, and a stopped job. A serious strike can injure or kill someone. Against that, potholing a few crossings costs a crew a day. This is one of the rare places in project management where the safe choice and the cheap choice are the same choice, and people still get it wrong because the cost of potholing is visible today and the cost of the strike is only probable.

Locates tell you what is underground. A different set of people control whether you may occupy the ground at all. On a road job in a Florida city, several of them have a say at once.

Suppose the crew reaches a fresh yellow gas mark while running two days behind. A valid ticket does not authorize machine excavation up to the paint. It tells you that a gas facility is approximately there. The readiness requirement is still to hand dig or vacuum within the tolerance zone and expose the facility before machine work continues. Schedule pressure does not alter the evidence required to proceed.

Commissioning and acceptance

A pipe in the ground is not an asset. It becomes one when it has been pressure tested, disinfected, cleared by a laboratory, connected, and accepted. Commissioning is where optimistic schedules go to die, because most of its duration is fixed by standards and biology rather than by effort. Run the Millpond clearance and see.

Notice what the simulation refuses to let you do. Even with everything passing first time, the clearance takes six days, because the hold time and the requirement for two samples taken 24 hours apart are set by standard, not by how hard the crew works. There is no crashing available here, which makes commissioning genuinely different from the rest of the schedule: adding people does not help. Chapter 06 taught you to crash the critical path. This is the part you cannot crash, and the correct response is to plan honest duration for it at the start, not to discover it in the final week.

Two words get used loosely at the end of a job and mean very different things in a contract. Getting them wrong is expensive.

Now move from individual rules to the actual project decision: are we ready to proceed? A readiness gate does not ask whether the team is busy, nearly finished, or under pressure. It asks whether the required condition and evidence exist for the next irreversible move. Select each Millpond decision, run it one line at a time, and notice how one missing blocker controls the result even when the rest of the work is complete.

How to read it: select a decision at the top, run each evidence gate, and read the final release or hold instruction only after every line has been examined.

Readiness feedback updates as each gate is run.

Conclusion: readiness is controlled by the first unresolved blocker, not by the percentage of work already completed.

All three decisions are hard for the same reason. Most of the work is ready. The missing item looks small beside everything that has been completed. Readiness control exists precisely for that moment. It keeps an unverified gas conflict, an incomplete clearance, or an unfinished handover from disappearing inside a percentage-complete story.

Leader's call

The pressure to put an asset into service before it has cleared is the most dangerous moment on a water project, and it will be presented to you reasonably, by tired people, with a good argument. There is no version of this where you accept a partial clearance to save four days. Say so out loud, early, and before anyone is standing in front of you at the end of a long week asking for an exception.

Assemble the readiness and clearance pack

Use the tracker to rehearse the evidence set. Each click moves an item from not started to draft, evidence noted, and reviewed. “Reviewed” should mean a named person checked the actual evidence against the applicable requirement. The tracker is a learning aid stored on this device. It is not an authorization to excavate, energize, place water in service, or release payment.

A good pack does not hide a missing blocker behind six completed rows. If conflict verification is not reviewed, excavation at that crossing is not ready. If laboratory clearance is not reviewed, the main is not ready for service. If record drawings and asset data are not reviewed, the project may be substantially complete but it is not ready for final acceptance.

Use the final gate against the readiness pack. Select every rule that protects the integrity of the release decision. This is not a count of completed documents. The pack must identify the governing requirement, retained evidence, authorized reviewer, and any blocker that prevents the next irreversible move.

What to carry out of this chapter

  • 1.
    The hierarchy of controls is a hierarchy for a reason. Elimination and engineering controls work even when people have a bad day. Personal protective equipment is the last line, not the first move.
  • 2.
    Call before you dig, and respect the tolerance zone. Marks give you approximate horizontal position and almost nothing about depth. Inside the zone, hand dig or vacuum.
  • 3.
    Several agencies control one street. Local right of way, state roads, railroads, and other utility owners each hold something you need. Railroad crossings belong on the critical path from day one.
  • 4.
    Commissioning cannot be crashed. Hold times and 24 hour sample intervals are fixed by standard. Plan honest duration, including one failed round, or you will report a delay for something entirely normal.
  • 5.
    Substantial completion is not final completion. The gap between them is where punch lists, record drawings, and training quietly die. Retainage is your last piece of leverage, so use it.

The leader's bottom line

Everything in this chapter is a place where the pressure runs one direction and the right answer runs the other. Your job is to take the pressure off before the moment arrives: price the potholing, schedule the real clearance time, design the trench out where you can, and make your position on exceptions known while everyone is calm. A project manager who only shows up at the moment of decision has already lost the argument.

Questions people ask at the readiness gate

Does a valid 811 ticket prove the excavation is safe?

No. It starts the locate process and records the notified area and response window. The crew must still review responses and marks, respect the tolerance zone, verify critical conflicts, maintain the ticket, and follow current state and local requirements.

Can schedule pressure justify machine digging near a marked line?

No. Schedule pressure does not change the uncertainty in the mark or the requirement that applies within the tolerance zone. Recover the schedule elsewhere instead of transferring schedule risk into strike risk.

Why cannot more people shorten the laboratory clearance sequence?

Parts of the sequence are waiting periods and required sample intervals. Additional labor may help prepare, flush, or coordinate, but it cannot make a required hold period or laboratory result occur sooner. Plan those fixed durations honestly.

Can operations use the asset before final completion?

Often, yes, when the contract requirements for substantial completion and safe operating use have been met. That does not erase the remaining punch list, records, training, asset data, or payment conditions. The contract and authorized decision govern the exact milestone.

Who has authority to release a readiness gate?

The governing contract, safety plan, utility procedure, permit, and law determine the authorized role. The project manager organizes the evidence and makes the gate visible, but should not assume authority assigned to a competent person, engineer, laboratory reviewer, operator, or contracting officer.

What changes by role

Foundation

Practitioner

Leader

Evidence boundary
