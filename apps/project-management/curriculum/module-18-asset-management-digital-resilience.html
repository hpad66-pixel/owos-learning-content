Chapter 18 · The next thirty years

Asset management, digital delivery, and resilience

Your project lasts two years. The asset lasts fifty. This chapter is about the difference: how the decisions you make during delivery determine what operations inherits, what it costs to run, and whether it still works on the worst day.

Asset management and the handover to operations

Asset management asks a question projects rarely ask: what will this cost to own? The tool for answering it is life-cycle cost, and the reason it matters is that procurement rewards the capital number while the utility pays the ownership number. Two real pump options for the wellfield. Drag the evaluation period.

The crossover lands at about seven years, and the pumps will run for thirty. Over those thirty years Option A totals $6.6 million against Option B's $4.95 million, so the bid that looked $450,000 cheaper costs the utility about $1.65 million more to own, almost all of it in energy. This is not an exotic finding, it is the normal shape of pump, motor, and treatment decisions, and it is why best value procurement exists. If your evaluation criteria only count the capital number, you have guaranteed the expensive answer in advance, and no amount of good project management afterwards recovers it.

When the asset is finished, operations inherits it. What they receive determines what they can do with it for the next thirty years.

Leader's call

Put life-cycle cost in the evaluation criteria before the bid goes out, not in the debrief afterwards. If your procurement scores only capital cost, you have already chosen the expensive option and simply have not paid for it yet. The second call is smaller and just as durable: make handover documents a deliverable with a date and money attached, because everything that is not a deliverable becomes optional the moment the schedule tightens.

Digital delivery: GIS, BIM and SCADA

Three systems come up constantly, they are frequently confused, and they answer genuinely different questions. Getting them straight matters because each one needs different data specified in your contract, and data not specified during delivery is data you will never have.

A rough way to hold them apart. GIS (Geographic Information System) is the map and the inventory, and it is where your asset data has to land for anyone to find it later. BIM (Building Information Modeling) is the model, most valuable during design and construction, especially for finding clashes on a congested treatment plant site before a contractor finds them with an excavator. SCADA (Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition) is the nervous system, telling operators what is happening right now. The project manager's job is not to run any of these. It is to make sure the contract requires data in the format each one needs, and that somebody checks it before final payment.

One warning that belongs in this chapter. SCADA is an operational technology network, and connecting it carelessly to the business network or the internet creates real safety exposure, because this is equipment that moves water and doses chemicals. Water systems have been probed and occasionally reached. If your project touches SCADA, cybersecurity is not an information technology afterthought to handle at closeout, it is a design requirement, and the people who own that system should be in the room from the start.

Resilience, sustainability, and One Water

Resilience is not reliability. Reliability asks how often things break; resilience asks whether service continues when something does. In water, the concept that carries most of this is the difference between the capacity you installed and the capacity you can count on. Set the demand, then take a unit out.

Installed capacity is 22 million gallons per day (MGD) against a peak day demand of 18, which reads as comfortable. Take the largest well out and firm capacity is 14, and you are 4 MGD short on the worst day of the year. This is the arithmetic behind a great deal of utility capital planning, and it is why a project that adds redundancy can be worth funding even though it serves no new customers and shows up on no growth projection. Firm capacity is the honest number. Installed capacity is the comfortable one.

Resilience planning generalizes the same move: assume the thing you depend on is unavailable, and see what remains.

That last step is the One Water idea, and it is the reason this platform is named after it. Utilities are organized into departments that manage drinking water, wastewater, stormwater and reuse separately, with separate budgets, separate plans and separate project lists. The water itself does not respect any of those boundaries. A stormwater project that recharges an aquifer is a water supply project. A reuse expansion is a wastewater project, a supply project, and a nutrient compliance project at the same time. The projects with the best returns in this industry usually sit across two departments, which is exactly why they are the hardest ones to get funded, and why somebody has to argue for them at the portfolio level. Chapter 19 is about that level.

What to carry out of this chapter

  • 1.
    Decide on life-cycle cost, not capital cost. The option that wins the bid opening frequently loses over the asset's life, and the utility pays the difference in energy for thirty years.
  • 2.
    Make handover a deliverable. Record drawings, asset data, manuals, training and the warranty register need dates, owners, and retainage attached, or they evaporate under end-of-project pressure.
  • 3.
    GIS, BIM and SCADA answer different questions. Where things are, how it was built, what it is doing now. Specify the data each one needs while the contractor is still on site.
  • 4.
    Plan against firm capacity. Installed capacity is the comfortable number. Firm capacity, with the largest unit out, is the one that decides whether service holds.
  • 5.
    One Water means the boundaries are administrative, not physical. The highest-return projects usually cross two departments, which is exactly why they struggle to get funded.

The leader's bottom line

Every choice in this chapter is a trade between a cost you pay now and a cost somebody else pays later, and later is where utilities live. Score life-cycle cost, contract the handover, and plan against firm capacity. The project team leaves at final completion. Operations is still there in 2056, working with exactly what you left them.
