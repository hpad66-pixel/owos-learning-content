Risk: managing what might happen

Every project is a bet on an uncertain future. Risk management is not eliminating uncertainty, it is deciding which uncertainties to act on, how much to spend on each, and what to hold in reserve for the ones you cannot see coming. We will score risks, price them, respond to them, and plan for the unknown.

1 / 5

What a risk actually is

A risk is an uncertain event that would affect the project if it happens. The word usually means bad news, but a risk can also be an opportunity. You capture them all in a risk register, and you find them through deliberate identification, like a premortem where the team imagines the project has already failed and asks why. Sort a few.

2 / 5

Qualitative: score and rank

Before you spend a dollar analyzing risks, you sort them fast on two simple questions: how likely is it, and how bad if it happens? That is qualitative analysis, and it lives on a probability-impact matrix. Tap each Millpond risk to see where it lands and what to do about it.

3 / 5

Quantitative: put a dollar on it

For the risks that matter, you go further and attach real numbers. The workhorse is Expected Monetary Value (EMV): probability times impact. It sounds abstract until you use it to make a decision. Work one out.

For whole-project uncertainty, you stack all the risks together and run a Monte Carlo simulation, exactly the finish-date distribution you built in Chapter 06. EMV prices one risk; Monte Carlo prices the whole cloud of them.

4 / 5

Responses and reserves

Once a risk is understood, you choose a response. For threats there are four: avoid (remove the cause), transfer (shift it to someone else), mitigate (reduce the probability or impact), or accept (live with it, with a plan). Opportunities have mirror images (exploit, enhance, share, accept). Match each move.

Even a well-managed project holds money back for what it cannot name. Contingency reserve covers the known unknowns, the risks you identified and accepted; it is the project manager's to spend. Management reserve covers the unknown unknowns, the true surprises; it sits outside the baseline and only the sponsor releases it.

5 / 5

Uncertainty and resilience

Risk analysis handles the things you can name. The harder frontier is genuine uncertainty, the unknown unknowns you could never have listed. You cannot register what you cannot imagine, so instead of predicting each one, you build resilience: buffers, flexibility, redundancy, and reserves that help no matter what specifically goes wrong.

This is why a schedule buffer, a management reserve, and a flexible delivery method are worth more than a longer risk list on a truly complex job. A longer list still only covers what you thought of. Resilience covers what you did not. On a simple, well-understood job, lean hard on the risk register. On a complex, first-of-its-kind job, invest in slack and adaptability, because the risk that gets you will not be on the list.

Leader's call

Match your approach to how knowable the job is. Known work: a tight risk register and lean reserves. Novel, complex work: real buffers, a healthy management reserve, and a delivery method you can adapt. The biggest risk on a hard job is always the one nobody wrote down.

What to carry out of this chapter

  • 1.
    A risk is uncertain, and can be good or bad. Capture threats and opportunities in a living register.
  • 2.
    Rank fast on probability and impact. The matrix turns a long list into a short action plan; top-right gets attention first.
  • 3.
    Price it with EMV. Probability times impact lets you compare risks and decide whether prevention is worth it.
  • 4.
    Respond and reserve. Avoid, transfer, mitigate, or accept; hold contingency for known risks and a management reserve for the unknown.

The leader's bottom line

Risk management is spending attention and money in proportion to exposure: hard on the likely-and-severe, lightly on the rest, and always holding reserve for what you could not foresee. The register handles the known; resilience handles the rest.
