Governance, integration and tailoring

A project does not run in a vacuum. The utility decides which projects happen and how they are watched (governance), the pieces have to knit into one plan (integration), and the amount of process has to fit the size of the job (tailoring). This chapter walks all three, on the Millpond Road program.

Who decides: governance and the PMO

Governance is the set of rules and decision rights around projects: who approves a project, who can stop it, and at which checkpoints. The everyday shape of it is the gate: a go or no-go decision between phases, where a sponsor or committee confirms the work is still worth continuing before more money goes out the door.

The office that runs governance day to day is the Project Management Office (PMO). Not every PMO does the same job. There are three common types, from lightest touch to heaviest.

Most utilities land on supportive or controlling, and drift toward controlling as grant money and regulatory reporting grow, because those require you to prove a consistent process. Directive PMOs show up in large programs or where a utility deliberately centralizes delivery. There is no best type. There is a best fit for how capable your teams are and how much oversight your funding demands.

Leader's call

Match the PMO to your teams and your money. Capable teams plus light oversight want supportive. Grant-funded, audited programs want controlling. Thin bench or high stakes wants directive. Picking a heavier PMO than you need just adds drag.

Governance also decides which projects run. Utilities split the capital list into two piles: work you have to do, and work you choose to do. Sort a few Millpond Road candidates.

For the discretionary pile, selection is a weighted score: rate each project against a few criteria, weight the criteria by what your utility values most, and rank them. Change the weights and watch the order shift. That is, in miniature, how a capital plan gets prioritized.

Making the pieces one: integration and the PM plan

Integration is the least visible and most important part of the job. It is the work of making all the separate pieces (scope, schedule, cost, risk, procurement) fit together as one plan, and keeping them coordinated when one of them moves. A project starts with a charter (permission to begin) and grows into a project management plan (PM plan) (the full how-to).

The PM plan is really a bundle of smaller plans. Match each subsidiary plan to the question it answers.

Here is why integration is the hard part. Each subsidiary plan is easy to write on its own. The difficulty is that they are all connected. A change to scope moves the schedule and the cost. A new risk can force a procurement change. The integrator's job is to see the whole board, so a change in one place does not quietly break another. That is exactly the iron triangle from Chapter 01, now scaled up to the whole plan.

Integration is easiest to feel when you poke it. Scope is one input. Add a couple of blocks to the Millpond job and watch the schedule, the cost, and the risk all move at once. That simultaneous movement is the thing integration manages, and the reason a scope change goes through change control instead of a quiet edit.

Right-sizing the process: tailoring

Tailoring means fitting the amount of process to the job. Running a valve-replacement the same way you run a treatment-plant expansion wastes everyone's time on the small job and under-serves the big one. Slide from a small job to a large one and see how the process should grow.

What to carry out of this chapter

  • 1.
    Governance is decision rights. Gates give someone the job of saying go or no-go before each expensive phase, so weak projects stop early.
  • 2.
    Match the PMO to your teams and money. Supportive advises, controlling enforces, directive runs the work. Heavier than you need is just drag.
  • 3.
    Integration knits the pieces into one plan. A charter authorizes; the PM plan (subsidiary plans plus baselines) is the how. When one piece moves, update the others together.
  • 4.
    Tailor the process to the job. Light for small and low-risk, full for large and uncertain. Start from the standard, trim deliberately, and write down why.

The leader's bottom line

Governance, integration, and tailoring are all one question: how much structure does this work actually need to succeed, and who owns the decision? Set that on purpose. Too little and projects drift; too much and good people spend their time feeding the process instead of building the job.
