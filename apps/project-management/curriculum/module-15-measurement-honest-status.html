Chapter 15 · Measurement

Measurement and honest status

Every project reports status. Far fewer report it honestly, and almost none report it in a way a decision-maker can act on. This chapter is about measuring the few things that matter, and telling the truth about them early enough to do something.

Metrics, KPIs, and what is worth measuring

A Key Performance Indicator (KPI) is not just any number you can collect. The test is simple: would this number ever change a decision? If not, it is decoration. And the most useful measures come in two flavors. A lagging indicator tells you what already happened; a leading indicator warns you about what is coming. You need both, but only one of them lets you act in time. Sort a few.

Honest status: the worst light, not the average

Most status reporting uses red, amber, green (RAG). The failure mode is so common it has a name: the watermelon, green on the outside and red at the core. It happens when someone averages the measures, or reports a feeling instead of evidence. Set the Millpond numbers and watch what an honest rollup does.

Notice what the rollup refuses to do. Three of the four measures are green or amber, and an average would land somewhere comfortable. But the cost number is genuinely at risk, and hiding it inside an average is how a project arrives at the end $200k over with everyone surprised. Honest status is not pessimism, it is precision: green where things are green, red exactly where they are red.

A status report is evidence-grounded when every claim points to something measurable. Here is the shape of a status a board can actually use.

Leader's call

Make it safe to report red. If your team believes a red status is a career risk, you will get green reports until the project fails, and you will lose the months when it was still fixable. Reward the person who raises the flag early, every time, and watch what you do the first time someone does.

What to carry out of this chapter

  • 1.
    Measure what changes a decision. If a number would never alter what you do, it does not belong on the dashboard.
  • 2.
    You need leading indicators. Lagging measures are accurate but too late; leading ones (near-misses, first-pass approvals) buy you time to act.
  • 3.
    Honest status takes the worst light. Averaging measures into a comfortable green is the watermelon, and it costs you the months when the problem was still cheap.
  • 4.
    Ground every claim in evidence, say what changed, and end with the decision you need.

The leader's bottom line

The value of a status report is measured in how early it lets someone act. Report the worst light, back every claim with a number, and make raising a red flag the safest thing a person on your team can do. Projects are rarely lost to bad news; they are lost to bad news that arrived late.
