Chapter 15 · Measurement

Measurement and honest status

Every project reports status. Far fewer report it honestly, and almost none report it in a way a decision-maker can act on. This chapter is about measuring the few things that matter, and telling the truth about them early enough to do something.

Metrics, KPIs, and what is worth measuring

A Key Performance Indicator (KPI) is not just any number you can collect. The test is simple: would this number ever change a decision? If not, it is decoration. The most useful measures come in two flavors. A lagging indicator tells you what already happened. A leading indicator warns you about what is coming.

Take safety as a worked example. Last quarter's recordable injuries are lagging because the harm has already occurred. Near-miss reports, housekeeping findings, and completed pre-task plans are leading because they show whether controls are working before the injury. You need both. The lagging measure tells you whether the outcome was acceptable. The leading measure gives you time to change the next outcome.

Honest status: the worst light, not the average

Most status reporting uses red, amber, green (RAG). The failure mode is so common it has a name: the watermelon, green on the outside and red at the core. Move each Millpond measure across its threshold and watch the overall rating. The model deliberately refuses to average a blocking red condition into a comfortable color.

How to read it: adjust one measure at a time, observe its individual color, and then compare that color with the overall project status.

Conclusion: a summary is honest only when its rollup rule preserves every condition that requires leadership action.

Notice what the rollup refuses to do. Three of the four measures are green or amber, and an average would land somewhere comfortable. But the cost number is genuinely at risk, and hiding it inside an average is how a project arrives at the end $200k over with everyone surprised. Honest status is not pessimism, it is precision: green where things are green, red exactly where they are red.

A dashboard is a claim about the project. Forensics means tracing that claim backward. You check the source date, definition, threshold, trend, and relationship to the reported color. Then you check whether the narrative and requested decision agree with the evidence. Work through four Millpond evidence packets.

The forensic habit is simple: never argue with a color in the abstract. Trace the claim to evidence and then trace the evidence to a decision. Sometimes the color is wrong. Sometimes the number is stale. Sometimes the narrative hides the condition. Sometimes everything is accurate and the report still fails because nobody asked for action.

Leader's call

Make it safe to report red. If your team believes a red status is a career risk, you will get green reports until the project fails, and you will lose the months when it was still fixable. Reward the person who raises the flag early, every time, and watch what you do the first time someone does.

Produce the one-page status brief

Build a brief from Millpond or a non-sensitive project. Begin with the rating and its reason. Name the source and status date. State what changed. Then make the request that turns the report into a management tool. The builder saves on this device only.

Read the preview once as the project manager and once as the board member. The project manager should be able to defend every claim. The board member should know what decision is needed and by when. If the brief satisfies only one reader, it is not finished.

Use the final gate to test whether your brief can support a real decision. Select the elements that must survive executive compression. The detailed workbook can sit behind the page, but the rating, source, change, consequence, accountable response, and requested decision must remain visible.

What to carry out of this chapter

  • 1.
    Measure what changes a decision. If a number would never alter what you do, it does not belong on the dashboard.
  • 2.
    You need leading indicators. Lagging measures are accurate but too late; leading ones (near-misses, first-pass approvals) buy you time to act.
  • 3.
    Honest status takes the worst light. Averaging measures into a comfortable green is the watermelon, and it costs you the months when the problem was still cheap.
  • 4.
    Ground every claim in evidence, say what changed, and end with the decision you need.

The leader's bottom line

The value of a status report is measured in how early it lets someone act. Report the worst light, back every claim with a number, and make raising a red flag the safest thing a person on your team can do. Projects are rarely lost to bad news; they are lost to bad news that arrived late.

Questions people ask about honest status

Does one red measure always make the whole project red?

Only when the course's stated rollup rule treats that measure as a project-level blocker. The point is not to use the worst color mechanically in every organization. The point is to define the rule before the report and never average away a condition that needs leadership action. A red permit on the critical path may control the whole project. A red housekeeping metric may require action without changing the overall rating.

Can contingency make a cost problem green?

No. Contingency may fund an approved response to uncertainty, but it does not change the cost-performance evidence that created the problem. Report the current condition honestly, explain the proposed contingency use, and obtain the required authority.

Why is zero near-miss reporting treated cautiously?

Because the number can mean two opposite things. It may mean controls are working, or it may mean people stopped reporting. Look at field observations, reporting behavior, pre-task planning, and trust before interpreting the number. The measure needs context.

How much evidence belongs in an executive status report?

Enough to support the claim and decision without burying the reader. Name the current measure, source, date, trend, owner, and consequence. Keep detailed calculations and records available behind the brief. The report is an index to the evidence, not a replacement for it.

Should a project manager wait for perfect data before reporting red?

No. Report the known condition, identify what remains unverified, and state when the missing evidence will arrive. A clearly labeled provisional red or amber is more useful than a confident green built on stale information.
