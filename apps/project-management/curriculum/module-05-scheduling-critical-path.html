Chapter 05 · Scope and schedule

The schedule and the critical path

A schedule is not a list of dates. It is a model of how the work connects, and it quietly tells you one thing that matters more than all the rest: which activities actually set your finish date. This chapter builds a schedule and finds that chain, the critical path, then loads cost onto it.

The Gantt: the schedule you can see

A schedule is built from activities, each with a duration, connected by dependencies (what waits on what). A milestone marks an important moment. Drawn out over time, that is a Gantt chart. Here is the Millpond schedule. Click any bar to see its details.

One more term. The baseline is the approved schedule you measure against later. You do not judge progress against your hopes, you judge it against the baseline.

Notice the shape already. The visible field work, excavate and lay pipe, is only four weeks. The two longest bars are design and material procurement, neither of which happens in the trench. That is the first lesson of scheduling: the work you can see is rarely the work that sets your finish date.

2 / 4

Dependencies and lag: the logic that links it

Dependencies come in a few types. Most are Finish-to-Start (FS): one thing must finish before the next starts. But not all. Start-to-Start (SS) lets work run in parallel, and Finish-to-Finish (FF) ties two finishes together. Sort a few.

You also adjust timing with lag and lead. A lag is a forced wait, like curing time after a concrete thrust block before you can pressurize the line. A lead is the opposite, an overlap you allow to save time. Lags are logic, not padding: they belong in the schedule because the work truly requires them, not because you want a cushion.

The critical path: what really sets the finish date

Here is the heart of scheduling. The Critical Path Method (CPM) finds the longest chain of dependent activities through the schedule. That chain is the critical path, and it sets your finish date. Everything else has float, room to slip without moving the end. Scroll back to the Gantt: the gold bars are the critical path. Here is how it is found.

Reading those steps is one thing. Watching them run is another. Step through the derivation below, or press Play. Each earliest date fills in on the forward pass, each latest date on the backward pass, then float appears and the critical chain lights up gold. This is the actual algorithm, not a picture of it.

On Millpond, the critical path does not run through the pipe crew. It runs through the 14-week material procurement. The permits, which everyone frets about, actually finish with five weeks of float to spare. Test your read of it.

Leader's call

Ask one question in every schedule review: what is on the critical path right now, and is it the long-lead procurement or a permit rather than the field work? Manage those, protect their float, and stop pouring urgency onto activities that have slack to spare.

4 / 4

Loading cost onto the schedule: the S-curve

Once the schedule is set, you spread each activity's cost across the weeks it runs. That is a cost-loaded schedule, and adding it all up over time gives the S-curve. It is slow at first (design, procurement), steep through construction, and flattens at the end. That shape is your planned cash flow, and later it becomes the baseline you measure earned value against.

Two reasons this matters beyond the picture. First, finance needs it: a utility has to know when the cash goes out to plan draws and rate impacts, and a lumpy or front-loaded curve is a warning sign. Second, it is the foundation for tracking. When you compare what you planned to spend (this curve) with what you have actually earned and spent, you get earned value, the subject of a later chapter.

What to carry out of this chapter

  • 1.
    A schedule is a network, not a list. Activities, durations, and dependencies, drawn as a Gantt and measured against a baseline.
  • 2.
    The critical path sets the finish date. It is the longest chain of zero-float activities. Delay any one and the whole job slips.
  • 3.
    Float tells you where the slack is. Spend management energy on the critical path; stop rushing activities that have room to spare.
  • 4.
    On utility jobs the driver is usually procurement or permits, not the pipe crew. Load cost onto the schedule and the S-curve shows you the cash flow.

The leader's bottom line

The finish date is not set by how hard the crews work. It is set by the critical path. Find it, protect it, and check every review whether it runs through a long-lead order or an approval rather than the field. That single habit prevents most schedule blowups.
