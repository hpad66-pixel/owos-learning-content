Chapter 19 · Above the project

Program and portfolio management

Everything so far has been about running one project well. That is not enough. A utility runs dozens at once, competing for the same money and the same crews, and the decisions that matter most happen above any single project: which ones exist at all, in what order, and whether the benefit anybody promised ever actually arrives.

Zooming out from one job

Millpond Road was never really about Millpond Road. It is one of three projects in a pressure zone improvement program: the main replacement, a new booster station, and a storage tank rehabilitation. None of them delivers the promised benefit alone. Meanwhile the utility has eleven other projects and one capital budget.

What you will be able to do

  • Tell a project from a program from a portfolio, and know which problem you are solving
  • Manage toward a benefit instead of a set of completion dates
  • Prioritize a capital plan defensibly when everything cannot be funded
  • See why the same crews constrain every project at once
  • Place your organization on a maturity ladder and name the next rung
1 / 4

Program management and the benefit

A project produces a deliverable. A program is a group of related projects managed together because coordinating them produces something none of them delivers alone. A portfolio is everything the organization has chosen to do, viewed together so money can be allocated across it. The distinction sounds academic until you watch a benefit fail to arrive. Set the three pressure zone projects.

This is the whole argument for program management in one picture. Three project managers can each deliver on time and under budget, and the utility still gets no benefit until month 22, because customers do not experience a main, a booster station, or a tank. They experience pressure, and pressure needs all three. Anything spent accelerating the main replacement is spent for nothing. The program manager's job is to find the driver and push there, which is the same critical path logic from Chapter 05 applied one level up, with benefits in place of activities.

2 / 4

Portfolio management and prioritization

At the portfolio level the question changes from "how do we deliver this" to "which of these do we do at all." Utilities have far more needed projects than money, so prioritization is not a bureaucratic exercise, it is the single most consequential decision the organization makes each year. The defensible way to do it is to agree the criteria and their weights before looking at the projects. Move the weights and watch the ranking rearrange.

Two things are worth noticing. First, the ranking is genuinely sensitive to the weights, which means the weights are where the real decision lives. Agreeing them in a calm room with your leadership, in advance, is worth more than any amount of careful scoring afterwards. Second, a scoring model does not remove judgment, it makes judgment visible and reviewable. When a council member asks why their neighborhood's project is eighth, "because growth is weighted 2 and compliance is weighted 5, and here is the model we adopted in November" is an answer. "Engineering judgment" is not.

Ranking tells you the order. The budget tells you where the line falls.

Leader's call

Set the weights in public and in advance, then let the model do the arguing. A prioritization your council helped weight is a prioritization your council will defend; one that arrives as a ranked list from engineering is one they will relitigate every year, project by project. The point of the model is not accuracy, it is that the conversation happens once, at the level of principles, instead of eleven times at the level of individual neighborhoods.

3 / 4

Resources across the whole portfolio

Every project in that plan draws on the same finite pool: inspectors, engineers, operations staff who must be present for tie-ins, and the two or three contractors who actually bid your work. Chapter 06 introduced resource leveling inside one schedule. At portfolio level the same problem appears across projects that have no idea the others exist.

The uncomfortable truth in that picture is that leveling does not create capacity, it moves work into the gaps and the portfolio takes longer. There are only three honest responses when demand exceeds supply: extend the schedule, add capacity by hiring or outsourcing inspection, or do fewer projects. Pretending all three projects can run at full speed with three crews is a fourth option that organizations choose constantly, and it resolves itself as quality problems, burnt-out staff, and slipped dates that nobody predicted but everyone could have.

4 / 4

Organizational maturity

Organizational Project Management (OPM) is the idea that all of this should work as one system rather than as habits that vary by whoever is running a given job. Maturity models describe the ladder. The rungs matter less than being honest about which one you are standing on.

One caution about maturity models. They are useful as a mirror and dangerous as a goal. An organization that sets out to reach level 4 produces documentation; an organization that sets out to know its real cost performance produces level 4 as a side effect. The rung is a description of a capability you actually have, not a thing to be awarded. If you take one action from this section, make it the level 4 one: start capturing what your projects really cost and really took, against what you estimated. Within about three years that data changes every estimate you produce, and Chapter 07 becomes a great deal easier.

What to carry out of this chapter

  • 1.
    Project, program, portfolio are three different problems. Deliver a thing, coordinate toward a benefit, choose what to do at all.
  • 2.
    A benefit arrives on the slowest component's date. Find the driver and push there. Accelerating anything else buys deliverables, not benefit.
  • 3.
    The weights are the decision. Agree them publicly and in advance, and the annual argument happens once at the level of principles instead of every year project by project.
  • 4.
    Leveling never creates capacity. More time, more capacity, or fewer projects. Refusing to choose picks the fourth option, which resolves as quality problems and burnt-out staff.
  • 5.
    Capture what projects really cost and really took. It is the one maturity move that pays for itself, because within a few years your estimates stop being optimism.

The leader's bottom line

Above the project, your leverage is in choosing and sequencing, not in delivering. Fund the driver, weight the criteria in public, and be honest about how many projects your crews can actually carry. A utility that runs eleven projects badly because it could not say no to three of them is not more ambitious than one that runs eight well. It is just slower, and more expensive.
