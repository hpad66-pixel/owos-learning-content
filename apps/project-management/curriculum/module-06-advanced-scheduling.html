Where durations come from

A schedule is only as good as its durations, and there is more than one way to get them. Pick the method that fits how much you know.

When a task is genuinely uncertain, one number is a lie. Three-point estimating uses three numbers, and PERT combines them into an expected value that leans on the most likely but respects the extremes. Build one for the Millpond controls tie-in.

Fitting the plan to the crews and the calendar

A schedule can be perfect on paper and impossible to staff. Resource loading adds up the crews each week demands. When a week needs more than you have, you are over-allocated, and the fix is leveling: shift work until every week fits. Toggle it.

The grid makes over-allocation visible instead of leaving it buried in activity assignments.

How to read it: Compare weekly demand with the actual resource cap before and after leveling.

What this proves: A schedule that requires unavailable crews is not an executable plan.

There is a gentler cousin of leveling called smoothing. Smoothing only moves work within its existing float, so it never pushes the finish date, but it also cannot fix a real over-allocation on the critical path. Leveling can fix anything, but it costs you time. Reach for smoothing first, and level only when you must.

Pulling the finish in: crashing versus fast-tracking

When the finish has to move earlier, you have two tools, and they cost different things.

One more idea worth knowing. The Critical Chain Method (CCM) notices that people pad every task with private safety, which then gets wasted. It strips that padding out and pools it into a single project buffer at the end, which the manager watches as one number. It is a different philosophy from the classic critical path, and worth reading about when task padding is your real problem.

Schedule risk: from one date to real odds

Add up the most-likely durations and you get a single finish date. The trouble is that almost nobody hits it, because tasks slip more often than they gain, and the slips pile up. To see the truth, you run the schedule not once but a thousand times, each time rolling a realistic duration for every task from its range. That is a Monte Carlo simulation, and it turns your plan into odds.

The distribution shows uncertainty as a range of possible finishes instead of a false single date.

How to read it: Run enough trials to see the difference between the deterministic date, the median, and the safer P80 commitment.

What this proves: A commitment date should match the consequence of being late and the evidence in the model.

Two numbers come out of that cloud. The P50 is a coin flip: half the runs finish by then. The P80 is the date you can put your name on, because only one run in five comes in later. The distance from your plan to P80 is the schedule risk you were carrying without knowing it.

Leader's call

Never commit to the plan number. It is the most optimistic case dressed up as a forecast. Commit to the P80, hold the difference as schedule contingency, and report against it. That is the difference between a date you defend and a date you apologize for.

What to carry out of this chapter

  • 1.
    Match the estimate to what you know. Analogous early, parametric with a rate, bottom-up when the design is firm, three-point when a task is genuinely uncertain.
  • 2.
    A plan you cannot staff is not a plan. Load the resources, and level (or smooth) until every period fits the crews you actually have.
  • 3.
    Compress with your eyes open. Crashing buys time with money; fast-tracking buys it with risk. Pick the one you can afford.
  • 4.
    Commit to odds, not hope. Run a Monte Carlo, commit to the P80, and carry the gap to the plan as schedule contingency.

The leader's bottom line

The single most valuable habit in this chapter: stop committing to the plan date and start committing to the P80. Every other technique here, three-point estimates, leveling, buffers, feeds that one honest number you can actually defend.

Questions people actually ask

Chapter 06 FAQ

Is P80 a guarantee?

No. It is a modeled percentile based on the ranges, dependencies, and assumptions supplied. Poor inputs can still produce a misleading result.

What is the difference between leveling and smoothing?

Leveling may extend the finish to respect resource limits. Smoothing rearranges work within available float so the finish does not move.

When should I crash a schedule?

Only after confirming the target activity is on the controlling path and that added resources can shorten it without unacceptable cost, safety, or quality consequences.

Why is fast-tracking risky?

It overlaps work that was planned sequentially, so downstream work may start before upstream information is stable and may need rework.

Who chooses the commitment percentile?

The accountable decision maker should choose it with project, operational, financial, and risk advice based on the consequence of missing the date.
