The five process groups

Every project, of any size, runs through five process groups. The trap is thinking they are sequential phases. They overlap: you plan while you execute, and Monitoring and Controlling runs the entire time, wrapping around everything else. Click through them.

2 / 3

The operating rhythm

Execution and control are not separate activities you do at different times; they are one weekly loop. A steady operating rhythm is what keeps a project honest between the big milestones. The method below is the loop that runs every week on a well-managed job.

3 / 3

Integrated change control

Changes are not the enemy; uncontrolled changes are. Integrated change control is the disciplined way to handle a request: nothing gets built, dropped, or repriced until you assess its full impact and someone with authority decides. Walk the flow.

The reason you assess all the baselines is the ripple you saw back in the governance chapter: a change to one thing moves the others. Feel it again, in the moment a change request lands.

Leader's call

Run a rhythm and control your changes. A steady weekly loop keeps small problems small, and a real change-control process keeps the baseline meaning something. The projects that lose control rarely lose it all at once; they lose it one unlogged change and one skipped status at a time.

What to carry out of this chapter

  • 1.
    The process groups overlap. Initiating, planning, executing, monitoring-and-controlling, and closing are not a straight line, and control runs the whole time.
  • 2.
    Run a weekly rhythm. Plan, do, check against the plan, adjust. Small corrections weekly beat big rescues later.
  • 3.
    Control every change. Log it, assess the full impact, decide with the right authority, and update all baselines together.

The leader's bottom line

Execution is not a burst of effort; it is a rhythm and a discipline. Keep the weekly loop honest and put every change through the process, especially the small ones, and the baseline you fought to build in the earlier chapters will still mean something at the end.

Create the change decision record

Read this phase gate from request through baseline update. The gate is not bureaucracy for its own sake. Each stage prevents the team from building a change before scope, schedule, cost, risk, and authority have been considered together.

How to read it: each gate answers a different question: Is the request defined, analyzed, authorized, communicated, and reflected in the controlled baseline?

  1. Log and define the request
  2. Analyze integrated effects
  3. Obtain an authorized decision
  4. Update baselines and records
  5. Communicate and verify execution

A change is controlled only when the decision and every affected baseline tell the same story.

Use the control board as a weekly reading pattern. Green means evidence supports plan, amber means a trigger needs action, and red means the baseline or outcome is no longer credible without an authorized response.

Questions people ask about executing and controlling

Are the five process groups the same as project phases?

No. Phases describe the product or delivery life cycle. Process groups describe the management work, and several run at the same time. Monitoring and Controlling continues while Millpond is planned, built, and closed.

Does every small field change need a board meeting?

No. The approved plan should delegate authority by threshold. The control still needs definition, impact review, an authorized decision, and updated records at the level the change requires.

Why update several baselines for one change?

Because scope, schedule, cost, risk, quality, permits, and operations are connected. A reroute that changes only the drawing leaves the schedule and cost reports describing a project that no longer exists.

Can work start while the price is still being negotiated?

Only under the contract and an authorized written direction that defines scope, basis, controls, and reservation of rights. A casual instruction creates cost and entitlement ambiguity.

What makes a weekly control meeting useful?

Evidence, thresholds, owners, and dated actions. A meeting that only reports activity creates conversation, not control.
