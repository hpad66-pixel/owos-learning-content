Chapter 10 · Quality and risk

Quality: building it right, not gold-plating it

Quality is not making the fanciest thing. It is delivering exactly what the requirements called for, provably. This chapter covers how you build quality in (assurance), how you check it (control), where to spend to get it, and what to do when something does not conform.

Assurance, control, and the plan

Two words that sound alike and mean different things. Quality Assurance (QA) is about the process: the procedures, training, and approved materials that prevent defects. Quality Control (QC) is about the product: the inspections and tests that catch defects. Assurance prevents, control detects. Sort a few.

Quality control runs on an Inspection and Test Plan (ITP), which lists every check on the job. Two kinds of checkpoint matter most. A hold point stops the work until an inspector signs off. A witness point invites the inspector but lets work continue. Walk the Millpond sequence from submittal through pressure testing. Watch where the work becomes hidden and compare that point with the placement of the hold gate.

How to read it: move left to right. A gold gate means the crew cannot proceed until the stated acceptance evidence is reviewed.

Conclusion: the strongest hold point sits immediately before backfill because that is when a visible, inexpensive correction becomes a buried, expensive failure.

How much should you spend on all this checking? There is a real answer, and it is not "as much as possible." The cost of quality is prevention and appraisal spend plus the cost of the failures that slip through. Move the prevention slider and watch three lines: planned quality spending, failure cost, and their total. The lowest point on the total line is the planning insight, not the highest inspection budget.

How to read it: prevention spending rises from left to right while expected failure cost falls. Read the dark total-cost curve to find the lowest combined cost.

Conclusion: under-investing leaves expensive defects, but unlimited inspection also wastes money. The useful target is the minimum total cost supported by the project risk and requirements.

When something does not meet spec, it gets logged as a Non-Conformance Report (NCR). The NCR is not a punishment. It is a controlled repair path. The record says what requirement was missed, what evidence proves the miss, who may decide the disposition, what corrective work is required, and what evidence closes the issue. A job with zero NCRs is often a job that is not looking very hard.

Before you diagnose the Millpond cases, put the repair path in order. The order matters because a crew cannot choose a convenient correction before the authorized engineer or owner has decided whether that correction is acceptable. A repair that works physically can still fail contractually when the approval and verification trail is missing.

Now work as the quality lead. Each case gives you an observed condition, but the first visible defect is not always the real management failure. Read the evidence, identify what failed, and choose the next move that repairs both the work and the control that allowed it.

The pattern across all three cases is the point of the clinic. A quality manager does not merely point at a bad object. You connect the object to the requirement, the process that produced it, the person authorized to decide, and the evidence that proves the repair. That is how an isolated defect becomes useful information instead of recurring rework.

Leader's call

Put your quality money at the front, in prevention and hold points, not at the end in rework. A joint inspected before backfill costs minutes; the same joint found after a road is repaved costs a project. And do not read zero NCRs as good news, read it as nobody looking.

Write the quality repair record

Use one of the clinic cases, or a non-sensitive case from your own work. Write the record so that a superintendent, engineer, operator, and auditor can all understand the same chain without calling you for the missing story. The builder saves only in this browser. Move the finished text into your utility's approved project system if you intend to use it.

A complete record should let a reviewer answer six questions without guessing: What was required? What failed? Who was authorized to decide? What changed? What proved the change worked? What remains open? If any answer is missing, the defect may be repaired in the ground while the quality system is still broken.

What to carry out of this chapter

  • 1.
    Quality is conformance, not gold-plating. Deliver exactly what the requirements asked for, and prove it.
  • 2.
    Assurance prevents, control detects. Build quality in with process; catch what slips through with inspection and test.
  • 3.
    Put hold points where defects get buried. The ITP checkpoint before backfill is worth a hundred after handover.
  • 4.
    There is a sweet spot. Spend enough on prevention to keep failures low, and treat every NCR as information.

The leader's bottom line

Quality is cheapest when it is prevented and most expensive when it is buried. Spend at the front, inspect at the points of no return, and log every non-conformance honestly. That is the whole discipline, and it pays for itself many times over.

Questions people ask after the inspection

Does opening an NCR mean the contractor is automatically at fault?

No. An NCR records that a requirement was not met. Responsibility, cost, and schedule entitlement may require a separate contract review. For example, a valve may be non-conforming because the approved drawing was wrong, because the supplier shipped the wrong item, or because the installer ignored the approved submittal. The record should establish the facts before it assigns blame.

Can an engineer approve a use-as-is disposition?

Only when that person has the required authority and enough evidence to evaluate the consequence. Use-as-is does not mean the defect disappeared. It means an authorized decision-maker accepted the condition with a documented technical and contractual basis. Local procedures and contract authority govern who can make that call.

Why not repair the defect first and complete the paperwork later?

Because the original condition is evidence. If the crew changes it before the condition, location, and requirement are recorded, the team may lose the ability to determine cause, responsibility, and the correct verification. At Millpond, photographing and locating the twisted gasket before disassembly preserves the evidence while the repair is still quick.

Is a project with many NCRs necessarily a poor-quality project?

Not by count alone. A high count can mean poor workmanship, strong inspection, or both. Look at severity, recurrence, age, closure quality, and cause. Ten minor items found and closed before burial may be healthier than one repeated defect hidden until startup.

Where should the project place hold points?

Place them immediately before work becomes hidden, irreversible, hazardous to correct, or expensive to reopen. Joint inspection before backfill is the obvious Millpond example. The hold point should protect a defined acceptance criterion, not stop work without a clear inspection purpose.
