How work gets delivered, and how it flows

Two decisions shape almost everything else on a job: the life cycle you run the work through, and the delivery method you use to hire and organize the people who do it. This chapter walks both, in plain terms, using one real job the whole way.

1 / 6

Two words to get straight first

People use these two words loosely on job sites, and it causes real confusion. A life cycle is the shape of the work over time: the phases you move through from the first idea to the day you hand it over. A delivery method is different. It is how you hire and organize the people, who designs, who builds, and who carries the risk.

Here is the easy way to keep them apart. The life cycle is the path the work walks. The delivery method is who you hire to walk it with you, and on what contract. The Millpond lift station runs the same phases (the same life cycle) whether you hire one design-build firm or a designer and a contractor separately (two different delivery methods). The phases do not change. Who you sign contracts with does.

Leader's call

Do not let anyone collapse these two into one decision. You pick how the work flows and, separately, who you contract with to do it. Getting one right does not save you if the other is wrong.

2 / 6

The spectrum: predictive to adaptive

Life cycles are not two boxes. They are a slider. A predictive life cycle (you may hear it called waterfall) plans the whole job first, then builds to the plan. An adaptive one (you may hear agile or iterative) works in short cycles and adjusts as it learns. Most real utility work sits somewhere between the two ends.

Millpond is a good example of why the slider matters. The civil and mechanical work (the wet well, the pumps, the generator) is well understood, so that part wants a predictive plan. The controls tie-in to the existing system is full of unknowns you will only find once you open the panel, so that part wants shorter loops. One job, two speeds. Leader's call

Match the life cycle to how much you actually know. Forcing a fixed, plan-it-all-up-front approach onto the uncertain part of a job is how you buy a pile of change orders.

Four ways to hire the work done

Now the delivery method: how you actually contract for the design and the build. Four show up on almost every utility program. Read each name in full once, then the table lets you compare them.

Design-Bid-Build (DBB) is the traditional path: design it fully, bid it, build it, in three separate steps. Design-Build (DB) puts design and construction under one contract with one firm. Construction Manager at Risk (CMAR) brings the builder in early to advise during design, then that builder commits to a price and constructs it. Progressive Design-Build (PDB) is a design-build team hired early on qualifications, developing the design and price with you step by step.

One more term you will see in those contracts: a Guaranteed Maximum Price (GMP) is a cap the builder promises not to exceed. It shows up in CMAR and PDB.

The table exposes the tradeoffs hidden behind delivery-method labels.

How to read it: Compare control, speed, price timing, and owner capacity across methods instead of searching for one universally best method.

What this proves: The best method is the one that fits the work, authority, market, and owner capability.

The rules you actually work under

Delivery choice is not a free pick. In Florida, how you buy design and how you buy construction are governed separately, and that shapes what is even on the table.

You hire your design engineer under the Consultants' Competitive Negotiation Act (CCNA), which means you pick engineering firms on their qualifications first and negotiate the fee after, not on low bid. Construction, by contrast, usually falls under competitive bidding rules that lean toward awarding to a qualified low bidder. Using an alternative method like Design-Build or CMAR generally needs specific authority your agency has to hold, so confirm what your procurement office actually allows before you promise a method to anyone.

This is why the same job gets delivered differently by two neighboring utilities. One has standing authority for Progressive Design-Build and uses it for anything with real unknowns. The next town over has to competitively bid construction and runs almost everything as Design-Bid-Build. Same pumps, same statutes, different delivery, because their procurement authority differs. Know yours before you design the approach.

6 / 6

What to carry out of this chapter

If you remember five things, remember these.

  • 1.
    Life cycle and delivery method are two different decisions. One is how the work flows, the other is who you contract with. Decide them on purpose, not by habit.
  • 2.
    Match the life cycle to how much you know. Predictive for the parts you can draw today, adaptive loops for the parts you will only learn in the field. Most real jobs are a mix.
  • 3.
    Gates exist to catch trouble before it gets expensive. The gate before construction is the cheapest place to find the gap you missed.
  • 4.
    Each delivery method trades something. DBB gives control and low bid but slow, late pricing. DB gives speed. CMAR and PDB give early builder input and a price cap on uncertain work.
  • 5.
    The rules bound the choice. CCNA sets how you hire the designer; competitive bidding and your alternative-delivery authority set how you hire the builder. Check them before you promise a method.

The leader's bottom line

Most delivery pain is a mismatch between how uncertain the work is, how you contracted for it, and what your rules allow. On Millpond, that points to CMAR or Progressive Design-Build: keep design control, get the builder in early on the risky controls tie-in, and lock a price you can defend.

Questions people actually ask

Chapter 02 FAQ

Are life cycle and delivery method the same thing?

No. The life cycle describes how the work is planned and learned. The delivery method describes the contractual relationships used to design and build it.

Is Design-Build always faster?

It can overlap design and construction, but actual speed depends on approvals, owner decisions, procurement, permitting, and the quality of the performance requirements.

When is adaptive work useful on a capital project?

Use short learning loops where conditions or interfaces cannot be known confidently in advance, while keeping safety, compliance, and construction controls explicit.

Can a utility choose any delivery method it prefers?

No. State law, local procurement rules, funding conditions, and delegated authority can limit the available choices.

Why write down the selection rationale?

Because the rationale preserves the decision when staff changes and lets later reviewers distinguish a deliberate tradeoff from habit.
