How work gets delivered, and how it flows

Two decisions shape almost everything else on a job: the life cycle you run the work through, and the delivery method you use to hire and organize the people who do it. This chapter lays out the choices in plain terms, with water examples, so you can pick the right ones on your next project.

1 / 5

Two words to get straight first

People use these two words loosely on job sites, and it causes real confusion. Let us pin them down.

A life cycle is the shape of the work over time: the phases you move through from the first idea to the day you hand it over. A delivery method is different. It is how you hire and organize the people, who designs, who builds, and who carries the risk.

Here is the easy way to keep them apart. The life cycle is the path the work walks. The delivery method is who you hire to walk it with you, and on what contract. A lift station rebuild can run the same phases (the same life cycle) whether you hire one design-build firm or a designer and a contractor separately (two different delivery methods).

2 / 5

The spectrum: predictive to adaptive

Life cycles are not two boxes. They are a slider. On one end you plan the whole job up front and follow the plan. On the other end you work in short loops and adjust as you learn. Most real utility work sits somewhere in between.

A predictive life cycle (you may hear it called waterfall) plans it all first, then builds to the plan. An adaptive one (you may hear agile or iterative) works in short cycles and adjusts. Slide the marker and see where a job lands.

3 / 5

The two shapes, side by side

Predictive work moves in a line through gates. Adaptive work moves in a loop that repeats. Seeing the two shapes next to each other makes the difference click.

4 / 5

Four ways to hire the work done

Now the delivery method: how you actually contract for the design and the build. Four show up on almost every utility program. Read each name in full once, then the table lets you compare them.

Design-Bid-Build (DBB) is the traditional path: design it fully, bid it, build it, in three separate steps. Design-Build (DB) puts design and construction under one contract with one firm. Construction Manager at Risk (CMAR) brings the builder in early to advise during design, then that builder commits to a price and constructs it. Progressive Design-Build (PDB) is a design-build team hired early on qualifications, developing the design and price with you step by step.

One more term you will see in those contracts: a Guaranteed Maximum Price (GMP) is a cap the builder promises not to exceed. It shows up in CMAR and PDB.
