Chapter 13 · People and leadership

Leading, negotiating, and doing it right

Authority gets you a title; leadership gets you a team that follows. This chapter is the soft skills that decide hard outcomes: leading field crews the way the moment calls for, negotiating so both sides can say yes, handling conflict on purpose, and holding the ethical line that a public trust demands.

The job and its people

The Millpond Road water main replacement. Same job. Now it is about leading the crews, negotiating a fair change-order price with the contractor, working through the inevitable conflicts, and keeping every dealing above board.

What you will be able to do

  • Match your leadership style to the team and the moment
  • Negotiate using interests and your BATNA, and find the zone of agreement
  • Choose a conflict-handling mode on purpose, not by reflex
  • Recognize and hold the ethical line a public project demands
1 / 4

Leadership: match the style to the moment

There is no one right way to lead. The best style depends on how ready the team is, an idea called situational leadership. A new crew needs direction; a seasoned one needs to be left alone. Slide from a green team to a veteran one and watch how the style should change. Notice it tracks the team stages from the last chapter.

2 / 4

Negotiation: finding the zone

Good negotiation is not winning; it is finding a deal both sides prefer to no deal. Two ideas make it work. Argue interests (what each side actually needs), not positions (the number they opened with). And know your BATNA, your best move if there is no deal, because that is your real power. Where the two sides' acceptable ranges overlap is the ZOPA, the zone of possible agreement. Drag the two limits and watch it appear and vanish.

3 / 4

Conflict: pick the mode on purpose

Conflict on a project is not a failure, it is normal, and how you handle it is a choice. There are five ways to respond, and each fits different moments: collaborating (find a win-win), compromising (split it), competing (stand firm), avoiding (let it go), and accommodating (give in). None is always right. Match a few.

4 / 4

Ethics: the line you do not cross

A public water project runs on public money and public trust, which raises the ethical bar. The core ideas are simple to state and hard in the moment: avoid conflicts of interest (and even their appearance), be honest in reporting, protect health and safety above schedule and cost, and never accept anything that could look like it bought your judgment. Test one.

Leader's call

Lead to the team in front of you, negotiate to interests and your BATNA, choose your conflict mode on purpose, and treat every ethical gray area as if it will be on the front page, because on a public project it can be. Reputation is the one project asset you cannot rebuild.

What to carry out of this chapter

  • 1.
    Lead the team you have. Direct a new crew, delegate to a seasoned one; the right style matches their readiness.
  • 2.
    Negotiate interests and BATNA. Argue what each side needs, know your walk-away, and aim for the zone of agreement.
  • 3.
    Choose the conflict mode. Collaborate when it matters to both, compete on non-negotiables, avoid the trivial, compromise to move on.
  • 4.
    Hold the ethical line. Avoid conflicts of interest and their appearance; public trust does not survive good intentions.

The leader's bottom line

Technical skill gets you into the room; leadership, negotiation, and integrity decide what happens there. Adapt to the team, bargain to interests, choose your conflicts, and never trade your reputation. These are the skills that outlast any single project.
