What a project is

Is it a project, or is it operations?

Here is the easiest way to tell them apart. A project has a finish line. You start it, work through it, and one day it is done and handed over. Replacing the Millpond Road main is a project. Operations are the opposite. They never finish. They just keep going, day after day, like treating the water or reading meters.

Why does this matter on day one? Because you run the two completely differently. A project needs a plan, a schedule, and a clear finish. Operations need a steady routine. When people treat a project like it is just a quick job, it drifts along with no real finish line and nobody watching the budget.

Leader's call

Keep capital projects and operations on separate books. Projects compete for money and finish; operations are the steady cost of service. Blend them and your capital plan gets muddy and the true cost of both is hard to see.

Use the finish-line test on each utility activity before revealing the explanation. Read the explanatory feedback before retrying so the correction becomes part of the method.

The three things you are always trading off

Every project balances how much work you take on (scope), how long you have (time), and how much you can spend (cost). You cannot change one without it showing up in the others. If the council adds two blocks to the Millpond Road job but will not move the deadline or the budget, something gives, and it is usually quality. People call this the iron triangle. Move the corners and watch it happen.

The diagram makes one point visible: scope, time, and cost cannot be changed independently.

How to read it: Read the three corners as linked commitments, then watch quality absorb an unsupported change.

What this proves: Changing one project constraint without changing another transfers pressure into quality or risk.

Notice the dot never leaves the triangle. Quality is not a fourth corner you can dial on its own. It is what lives in the middle and absorbs the pressure when the three corners fight. That is why "we will just hold quality" is never a real plan. Quality is the result of the other three, not a separate lever.

The phases a project moves through

A project officially begins with a charter, the one-page go-ahead. From there it moves through a set of phases called the life cycle. Between phases are gates, simple go or no-go checkpoints. Click a phase to see what happens in it, on the Millpond Road job.

Is the project even worth doing?

Before a project gets its charter, someone has to ask the honest question: is this worth the money? The answer is the business case. You do not need to be a finance person to build one. Here is the method.

Now run the real numbers on the Millpond Road main. The term Net Present Value (NPV) and the Benefit-Cost Ratio (BCR) sound like finance jargon, but the calculator does the math. Slide the inputs and watch a curve of your money over time and a bar chart of spend versus payback update live.

The calculator converts abstract finance terms into a visible decision about value over time.

How to read it: Read the initial investment below zero, the rising benefit curve, and the point where cumulative value crosses the baseline.

What this proves: A positive business case depends on stated assumptions, not on project enthusiasm.

Leader's call

For a utility that runs on rates, the business case is a rate conversation. A positive NPV over the life of the pipe is how you explain to a board, and to the customers paying the bill, why the money is worth spending now. And remember the other side: waiting until the old main finally breaks has its own cost, usually a bigger one.

One piece of that calculation is worth seeing on its own: discounting. Why is a dollar saved twenty years from now worth less than a dollar today? Slide the years and the rate and watch a future dollar shrink to its value in today's money.

Estimate payback from cost divided by annual saving, then compare your reasoning with the worked answer. Read the explanatory feedback before retrying so the correction becomes part of the method.

Lock it in

A couple of quick ways to make the words stick. First, flip through the key terms.

What to carry out of this chapter

  • 1.
    A project has a finish line; operations just keep going. You manage the two differently, and you fund them differently.
  • 2.
    Scope, time, and cost are tied together. Change one and another must move, or quality (the dot in the middle) pays for it.
  • 3.
    A charter starts a project and the life cycle carries it from kickoff to closeout, with a go or no-go gate between phases.
  • 4.
    The business case decides worth before you spend a dollar: NPV positive, a payback you can live with, and a benefit-cost ratio above 1.

The leader's bottom line

Two questions settle most of Chapter 01. Does it end? If yes, run it as a project. Is it worth it? If the NPV is positive over the asset's life, you can defend the spend to a board and to the ratepayers, and deferring it usually costs more.

Questions people actually ask

Chapter 01 FAQ

Can recurring work ever be managed as a project?

Yes. A one-time improvement to recurring work can be a project because the improvement has a finish line. The routine that remains afterward is operations.

Does a positive NPV automatically authorize the work?

No. It supports the value case, but authorization also depends on risk, affordability, legal authority, capacity, and competing priorities.

Why is quality shown inside the constraint model?

Quality reflects how well the promised scope is delivered within the time and cost commitments. If those commitments conflict, quality and risk usually absorb the pressure.

What if the benefit is public health rather than cash savings?

State the benefit and the evidence honestly. A utility business case can include avoided risk and service outcomes, but those should not be disguised as precise cash when the evidence does not support it.

Who owns the assumptions in the business case?

The sponsor owns the decision, while named technical, financial, operational, and project roles should own the assumptions they are qualified to support.
