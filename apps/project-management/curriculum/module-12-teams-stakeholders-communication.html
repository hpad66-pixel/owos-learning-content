Chapter 12 · People and leadership

The team, the stakeholders, and the message

Projects are run by people and judged by people. This chapter covers building a team that actually performs, mapping the stakeholders who can make or break the job, and planning communication so the right people hear the right thing without everyone drowning.

The job and its people

The Millpond Road water main replacement. Same job. Now we look past the schedule and the dollars to the people: the crew and staff who build it, the council, regulator, and residents who care about it, and how we keep them all informed.

Building the team

A group of good people is not yet a team. Teams move through predictable stages, named by Tuckman: forming, storming, norming, performing. The mistake is expecting performance in week one; the storming stage is normal, not a failure. Walk the stages.

Even a performing team stalls when nobody knows who owns what. A RACI chart fixes that by tagging every task with who is Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, and Informed. The key rule is exactly one Accountable person per task. The chart helps, but the real test happens in conversation. People must be able to say what they own, what they decide, and when they need someone else's input.

Listen to three short Millpond conversations. In each one, choose the project manager's response. A good response does more than name a RACI letter. It clarifies the decision, the owner, and the next exchange between people.

Conversation feedback appears after each response.

Notice the pattern. The useful answer names a real decision and then assigns the four roles around it. “Engineering is Accountable” is too broad. “The design manager is Accountable for approving the revised crossing detail before it is issued for construction” can be acted on and checked.

Stakeholders: who can make or break it

A stakeholder is anyone who can affect the project or is affected by it. You cannot engage them all the same way, so you map them by two things: how much power they have over the project, and how much interest they take in it. Tap each Millpond stakeholder. Notice that the position suggests an engagement intensity, but the detailed strategy still depends on the real decision and relationship.

How to read it: power rises from bottom to top and interest rises from left to right. Select a numbered stakeholder to see the corresponding engagement posture.

Conclusion: engagement effort follows practical influence and project impact, not job title alone.

On a public water job, the residents in the bottom-right, interested but with little formal power, are the ones most often mishandled. Ignore them and a routine main replacement becomes a packed council meeting. Keep them genuinely informed and they can become your advocates. That is what public involvement is really for.

The map does not assign a message by itself. You still have to choose a strategy that fits the stakeholder's power, interest, impact, and history with the utility. Work through the school principal's situation before you move on.

The strongest strategy is not “more communication.” It is a specific relationship: involve the principal in the traffic decision, agree on what will be communicated, name who can resolve a morning problem, and keep the utility accountable for the promise.

Communication: why it gets hard so fast

Here is a number every project leader should understand. The paths of communication in a team are not proportional to its size, they grow much faster. Move the team-size control and watch the network fill in. The visual counts every possible pair, which explains why adding one person adds several new coordination paths rather than one.

How to read it: every dot is a person and every line is a possible one-to-one communication channel. Increase the team size and compare the line count.

Conclusion: larger teams need planned routes, owners, and cadences because informal all-to-all communication becomes unmanageable.

Because you cannot let everyone talk to everyone, you plan communication deliberately: who needs what, how often, and in which direction. Some is push (you send it: status reports, notices), some is pull (they fetch it: a project website, a shared drive), and some is interactive (two-way: meetings, calls). Match the method to the message, and set a cadence people can rely on.

A four-hour shutdown requires more than choosing one channel. Build the communication set that covers direct notice, confirmation, field change, and a place for questions. Select every action that belongs in the plan.

Leader's call

Two habits prevent most people problems. Map the stakeholders before you need them (know who to manage closely and who to keep satisfied), and structure communication instead of letting it sprawl. A ten-person job free-for-all is 45 channels of noise; a planned one is a handful of clear ones.

The complete set is deliberately smaller than “tell everyone everything.” It controls one promise, gives it to the people who need it, and creates a route for exceptions. That is a communication plan. A larger mailing list is not.

Write one stakeholder engagement brief

Choose one stakeholder who can materially affect a utility project. Do not write “keep informed.” Write the actual decision, concern, message, channel, cadence, owner, and escalation route. The output should be clear enough that another project manager could carry the relationship for a week without inventing the strategy again.

Read the preview as if you were the stakeholder. Can you tell what is changing, why it matters to you, when you will hear from the project, and who can solve a problem? Then read it as the project sponsor. Can you tell who owns the relationship and when escalation is required? If both answers are yes, the brief is doing useful work.

Review the brief as a handoff between project leadership and the person who will manage the relationship. Select the elements that make the strategy executable. A generic instruction such as “communicate regularly” is not enough because it leaves the decision, owner, timing, and escalation route unspecified.

What to carry out of this chapter

  • 1.
    Teams storm before they perform. Expect the friction stage and lead through it; clarify every role with RACI, one Accountable each.
  • 2.
    Map stakeholders by power and interest. Manage the high-high closely, keep the powerful-but-uninterested satisfied, and turn interested residents into advocates.
  • 3.
    Communication scales badly. Channels grow as n(n-1)/2, so structure it: match push, pull, and interactive to the message, on a reliable cadence.

The leader's bottom line

The hardest risks on a utility project are usually people, not pipe. Know your stakeholders before you need them, name one owner for everything, and plan communication so the right people hear the right thing early. That prevents the failures no schedule can.

Questions people ask about the people side

Does every task really need a full RACI chart?

No. Use RACI where ownership or handoffs are genuinely unclear or consequential. A small field task may need one named owner and a clear acceptance point, not a four-column chart. The value is clarity, not paperwork.

Can the same person be Responsible and Accountable?

Yes, especially on a small project. The important rule is that the Accountable role is singular and has the authority to own the outcome. On a small valve replacement, the project manager may both prepare and own the shutdown plan. On a large program, those roles are more likely to be separated.

What if a stakeholder has little formal power but can create major delay?

Then the map should reflect their practical influence, not just their title. Residents near Millpond may not issue a permit, but organized complaints can change work hours or trigger council intervention. Map the consequence they can create and engage them accordingly.

Is more communication always safer?

No. More uncontrolled messages can create conflicting promises. Safer communication means one approved fact pattern, a clear audience, an appropriate channel, a reliable cadence, and an exception route. Three aligned messages are better than thirty improvised ones.

Who should own communication with an upset stakeholder?

Give the relationship one named owner who has access to the people with decision authority. That person does not need to answer every technical question personally. They need to keep the promise, coordinate the answer, and make sure the stakeholder is not passed from office to office.

What changes by role

Foundation

Name the decision, the stakeholder, and the difference between doing, owning, advising, and receiving information.

Practitioner

Run the role conversation, map practical influence, and build the message, cadence, owner, and escalation route.

Leader

Resolve competing authority, protect one accountable owner, and respond early when trust begins to erode.

Evidence boundary

The Millpond conversations are instructional scenarios. Real stakeholder commitments must use current project facts, approved public-information procedures, accessibility and language requirements, emergency protocols, and the utility's authorized spokespersons.
