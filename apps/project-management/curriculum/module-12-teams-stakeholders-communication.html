Chapter 12 · People and leadership

The team, the stakeholders, and the message

Projects are run by people and judged by people. This chapter covers building a team that actually performs, mapping the stakeholders who can make or break the job, and planning communication so the right people hear the right thing without everyone drowning.

The job and its people

The Millpond Road water main replacement. Same job. Now we look past the schedule and the dollars to the people: the crew and staff who build it, the council, regulator, and residents who care about it, and how we keep them all informed.

What you will be able to do

  • Recognize the stages a team goes through, and clarify roles with RACI
  • Map stakeholders by power and interest, and engage each the right way
  • Plan communication, and understand why it gets harder fast as the team grows
1 / 3

Building the team

A group of good people is not yet a team. Teams move through predictable stages, named by Tuckman: forming, storming, norming, performing. The mistake is expecting performance in week one; the storming stage is normal, not a failure. Walk the stages.

Even a performing team stalls when nobody knows who owns what. A RACI chart fixes that by tagging every task with who is Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, and Informed. The key rule: exactly one Accountable per task. Sort a few.

2 / 3

Stakeholders: who can make or break it

A stakeholder is anyone who can affect the project or is affected by it. You cannot engage them all the same way, so you map them by two things: how much power they have over the project, and how much interest they take in it. That is the same matrix idea from the risk chapter, applied to people. Tap each Millpond stakeholder.

On a public water job, the residents in the bottom-right, interested but with little formal power, are the ones most often mishandled. Ignore them and a routine main replacement becomes a packed council meeting. Keep them genuinely informed and they become your advocates. That is what public involvement is really for.

3 / 3

Communication: why it gets hard so fast

Here is a number every project leader should feel in their bones. The paths of communication in a team are not proportional to its size, they grow much faster. Add people and the channels explode. Watch it.

Because you cannot let everyone talk to everyone, you plan communication deliberately: who needs what, how often, and in which direction. Some is push (you send it: status reports, notices), some is pull (they fetch it: a project website, a shared drive), and some is interactive (two-way: meetings, calls). Match the method to the message, and set a cadence people can rely on.

Leader's call

Two habits prevent most people problems. Map the stakeholders before you need them (know who to manage closely and who to keep satisfied), and structure communication instead of letting it sprawl. A ten-person job free-for-all is 45 channels of noise; a planned one is a handful of clear ones.

What to carry out of this chapter

  • 1.
    Teams storm before they perform. Expect the friction stage and lead through it; clarify every role with RACI, one Accountable each.
  • 2.
    Map stakeholders by power and interest. Manage the high-high closely, keep the powerful-but-uninterested satisfied, and turn interested residents into advocates.
  • 3.
    Communication scales badly. Channels grow as n(n-1)/2, so structure it: match push, pull, and interactive to the message, on a reliable cadence.

The leader's bottom line

The hardest risks on a utility project are usually people, not pipe. Know your stakeholders before you need them, name one owner for everything, and plan communication so the right people hear the right thing early. That prevents the failures no schedule can.
