Chapter 20 · Capstone

Run the whole job: you decide, Droobi does

Nineteen chapters of method, applied once, end to end, on a job that behaves like a real one. At each gate you make the call. The mechanical work, the arithmetic, the drafting, the document assembly, gets done for you. What is left is the part that was always the actual job.

Full is the whole lesson. Plain trims to the core. Leader shows only the call you have to get right. Try the buttons, the page changes. Hover any dotted term for a plain definition.

⚑

The job, from the beginning

Cypress Creek lift station, rated capacity 2.5 million gallons per day. Built in 1978. Two pumps, both original. It has overflowed twice in three years during heavy rain, and one of those events was reported to the state. The area it serves has grown by about 30 percent since it was designed. You have just been handed it.

What this capstone asks of you

  • Choose a delivery approach and defend the choice
  • Take the decisions at each gate, in the order they actually arrive
  • Recognize which work is yours and which should be delegated to an agent
  • Write the honest status when the job goes sideways

The Cypress Creek job, gate by gate

Start where every project should start and almost none does: not with a solution, but with the question of what problem you are solving. Two overflows and 30 percent growth could mean several different projects.

The evaluation comes back after a wet season. The station's pumps are near end of life but functional. Peak wet weather flow reaches about 3.8 MGD against a firm capacity of 1.4 MGD, because with one pump out the station cannot handle it. Roughly 40 percent of that peak is inflow and infiltration (I&I): rainwater getting into a sewer built for sewage. So there are two candidate projects and they are very different sizes.

You carry forward I&I reduction plus a station sized for a reduced peak, with storage. Now the money. The project needs about $4.6 million and the annual capital budget is already committed.

Notice the shape of the last three decisions. Not one of them was an engineering question. They were about defining the problem, choosing among alternatives with different lifetime consequences, and trading money against time against risk. That is what the job is, and it is the part no tool does for you.

Design and permitting run. Construction starts. In month nine, the thing that always happens, happens.

The claim is valid under the differing site conditions clause. Negotiated, it settles at $265,000 and four weeks, which is $25,000 over your remaining contingency and consumes every day of float on the corrective schedule. Nothing has technically failed. Everything is now tight. This is the exact moment where status reporting either earns its keep or quietly fails.

Every gate in this chapter came down to something Chapters 01 through 19 covered: define before you solve, choose alternatives on life-cycle terms, back-plan from the compliance date, establish entitlement before negotiating, and report the worst light early. None of it required you to compute anything.

Where the agent fits

The reason to end this course with an agent is not novelty. It is that the work in the job splits cleanly into two piles, and most project managers spend far too much of their week in the wrong one. Sort the Cypress Creek tasks.

The pattern is consistent. Everything in the delegate column has a checkable right answer; everything in the yours column requires someone to be accountable for a judgment. This is why the arrival of capable agents does not shrink the project manager's role, it concentrates it. The arithmetic, tracking, and assembly that filled the calendar were never the value you added. They were the tax you paid to get to the decisions, and the decisions are where the whole job lives. A project manager who was mostly a spreadsheet operator should be concerned. One who was mostly making the calls in this chapter has just had their week handed back.

Leader's call

Delegate the mechanical work aggressively, and never delegate the accountability. An agent can tell you the forecast at completion is $4.87 million and the float is zero. Only a person can walk into a board meeting and say the project is red. If you find yourself hoping a tool will make the uncomfortable call for you, that is the clearest possible signal that the call is yours.

What to carry out of this capstone

  • 1.
    Define the problem before you accept a solution. "Replace the pumps" arrived as a diagnosis and was really a guess. The evaluation that felt like delay was the cheapest decision on the job.
  • 2.
    Alternatives are chosen on lifetime terms. Building for the measured peak would have meant pumping and treating rainwater for fifty years.
  • 3.
    Establish entitlement before you negotiate. Valid, entitled, and priced are three separate questions, and answering them out of order costs money either way.
  • 4.
    Report the worst light while it is still fixable. Zero contingency and zero float on a regulatory deadline is red, whatever the change order status says.
  • 5.
    Delegate what is checkable, own what is accountable. The mechanical work was never the value. It was the tax paid to reach the decisions.

The leader's bottom line

Twenty chapters reduce to something fairly simple. Projects are decided early and delivered late, and the decisions that matter are made by people who are accountable for them. Everything else in this course, the critical path, earned value, the risk register, the status report, exists to get the right information in front of that person while there is still time to act on it. Build the habit of being that person, and the tooling is a detail.
