An estimate is a range, not a number

The single most misunderstood thing about a cost estimate is that it is never one number, it is a range, and the range depends entirely on how far along the design is. The industry names these levels with AACE estimate classes, from Class 5 (a concept sketch) to Class 1 (a complete design ready to bid). Slide through them and watch the range close in on the number.

Three add-ons turn a raw estimate into a usable one. An allowance covers work you know is coming but cannot price yet. Escalation accounts for inflation between today and construction. And contingency covers the known unknowns, the things that usually go wrong but you cannot name yet.

From estimate to budget: the cost baseline and the CIP

Approve an estimate with its contingency and spread it over the schedule, and you have a cost baseline, the number you measure actual spending against. But first, one distinction that trips up every new utility PM: capital versus operating money.

A capital cost builds or replaces a long-lived asset. An O&M cost (Operations and Maintenance) is the recurring cost of running the system. They come from different budgets and are funded differently, so sort them right.

Classify each cost by how it should be treated before building the baseline. Read the explanatory feedback before retrying so the correction becomes part of the method.

Capital projects live in the Capital Improvement Plan (CIP), a multi-year, prioritized list. The hard truth of a CIP is that you can never fund everything at once. Slide the annual budget and watch which projects make the cut and which wait.

The gates show that a capital plan is a sequence of decisions, not a wish list.

How to read it: Move through prioritization, affordability, funding, authorization, and annual revalidation before treating a project as committed.

What this proves: A funded year is credible only when scope, estimate maturity, financing, and rate impact align.

Paying for it, and what it does to the bill

Capital money comes from a few places, and the cheaper it is, the more strings it carries. Click each source.

Whatever the source, debt has to be repaid, and that repayment shows up on customer bills. The rate impact is the number a board and the public actually care about. Work it out for Millpond.

The curve translates a capital decision into the customer scale leaders must explain.

How to read it: Change project cost, financing terms, and customer count, then watch the monthly bill consequence move.

What this proves: Affordability belongs in project selection, not only in the final rate hearing.

One compliance note that catches utilities off guard. The moment you take federal money, whether SRF, WIFIA, or a grant, strings attach to the construction itself. Davis-Bacon prevailing-wage rules, Buy America material sourcing, and extra reporting all apply. They can raise the construction cost and the administrative load, so cheap money is not entirely free. Factor that in before you assume the SRF loan is the obvious choice.

Leader's call

Two numbers decide a capital project at the board table: the cost baseline you can defend (built from a Class 3 or better estimate with contingency) and the rate impact per customer. If you walk in with a Class 5 guess and no per-bill number, you will not get a yes, and you should not.

What to carry out of this chapter

  • 1.
    An estimate is a range set by the design. Class 5 can be off by half; commit budgets from Class 3 or better, with contingency and escalation.
  • 2.
    Keep capital and O&M separate. The cost baseline is your time-phased budget, and capital projects compete in the multi-year CIP.
  • 3.
    Cheaper money carries more strings. SRF and WIFIA beat bonds on rate, but bring Davis-Bacon, Buy America, and reporting.
  • 4.
    Know the rate impact. Debt service divided across accounts is the per-bill number the board and the public actually weigh.

The leader's bottom line

You do not win capital money with a number, you win it with a defensible cost baseline and an honest rate impact, funded from the cheapest source you can live with the strings of. Estimate quality and the per-bill number are the whole game at the board table.

Questions people actually ask

Chapter 07 FAQ

Is contingency the same as padding?

No. Controlled contingency addresses identified and residual uncertainty with a stated basis and authority. Padding is hidden and cannot be governed.

Why show an estimate range instead of one number?

The range communicates uncertainty appropriate to design maturity. A single number can imply precision the evidence does not support.

Does a grant make a project free?

No. Grants may require match, compliance, administration, schedule, and long-term operating commitments. The utility still owns the asset consequences.

What belongs in lifecycle cost?

Include capital delivery plus material operating, maintenance, renewal, financing, and disposal consequences over the decision horizon.

How should rate impact be communicated?

State assumptions, customer classes, timing, financing, existing revenue needs, and the difference between this project's incremental effect and the total rate plan.
