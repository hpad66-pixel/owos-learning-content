Chapter 04 · Scope and schedule

Getting the scope right

Most projects that blow their budget did not fail during construction. They failed at the start, when the scope was fuzzy and the requirements were vague. This chapter shows how to pin down what a job actually has to do, and how to break that into work you can plan, price, and accept.

Requirements: what the job must actually do

A requirement is a specific thing the finished job has to do or meet. On Millpond Road they come in a few flavors, and it helps to know which is which, because you gather and verify them differently. Sort a handful below.

Classify each requirement before deciding who should validate it. Read the explanatory feedback before retrying so the correction becomes part of the method.

Feedback appears after you answer.

The single most common mistake is a requirement you cannot test. "Good water pressure" is not a requirement, it is a wish. Two people will read it differently and nobody can sign it off. Compare.

Two more terms before the method. Elicitation is the work of drawing requirements out of people, operators, engineers, regulators, rather than waiting for them to arrive. Traceability is being able to follow each requirement forward to the design that meets it and the test that proves it. Here is how to do both.

Why traceability matters so much: at the end of a job, someone asks "did we get everything the community was promised?" Without a traced list, you are guessing. With one, you walk the list, point to each test result, and prove it. It is also how you catch a requirement that quietly fell out during design, while it is still cheap to fix.

Leader's call

Spend real time here, before design. A requirement missed at the start costs a little to add in design, a lot to fix in construction, and a fortune to discover after startup. The cheapest place to get the scope right is the beginning.

Scope: breaking the job into buildable pieces

Once you know what the job must do, you write it down as scope. The scope statement says exactly what the project will deliver and, just as important, what it will not. Then you break that scope into a tree called the Work Breakdown Structure (WBS). Each branch splits the work until you reach a work package, small enough to estimate, assign, and track. Click through the Millpond WBS.

The tree shows how scope becomes manageable work without losing the parent deliverable.

How to read it: Read from the project deliverable down to work packages. Each branch should describe complete work, not a department or an activity list without an output.

What this proves: A WBS controls scope by making the whole deliverable visible and assignable.

Two rules make a WBS useful. First, it is built on deliverables (things you produce), not activities, so nothing gets double-counted. Second, every level rolls up completely: the children of a box add up to the whole box, no more and no less. That "100 percent" rule is what lets you trust that the WBS covers the whole job and only the job.

See the rule work. Put an estimate of hours on each work package (the leaves) and watch them roll up the tree to the project total. Change any one number and every level above it updates, with no gaps and no double counting.

The rollup reveals where a requirement can be lost between conversation and handover.

How to read it: Follow one requirement from need to design, work package, test, and acceptance evidence.

What this proves: A requirement is controlled only when its acceptance evidence can be traced.

The WBS is also your best defense against scope creep: work that gets added without adjusting the time or the budget. If a request is not on the WBS, it is a change, and a change gets priced and approved, not just absorbed. Sort what belongs.

What to carry out of this chapter

  • 1.
    A requirement must be testable. A number and a condition, not an adjective. If you cannot measure it, you cannot accept it.
  • 2.
    Trace every requirement to the design that meets it and the test that proves it. That trace is what protects you at closeout.
  • 3.
    The WBS breaks the job into work packages. Built on deliverables, rolling up completely, so nothing is forgotten and nothing outside the job sneaks in.
  • 4.
    Scope creep is change without a decision. If it is not on the WBS, price it and run it through change control, do not absorb it.

The leader's bottom line

Scope is where projects are won or lost, long before the first shovel. Write the requirements so they can be tested, break the job into a WBS that rolls up to 100 percent, and treat every new ask as a priced change. Do that and the schedule, cost, and risk chapters that follow all sit on solid ground.

Questions people actually ask

Chapter 04 FAQ

What makes a requirement testable?

It states a measurable condition, operating context, acceptance method, and responsible source without relying on words such as adequate or user-friendly.

Is the WBS the same as the schedule?

No. The WBS defines all deliverable-oriented scope. The schedule arranges the activities needed to produce that scope in time.

Can operations add a requirement during construction?

They can identify a need, but the team must evaluate and authorize it through change control before treating it as committed scope.

How much traceability is enough?

Use enough to connect material needs to design, procurement, construction, testing, and acceptance. The depth should follow consequence and complexity.

Why record the source of a requirement?

The source shows who can clarify intent, whether the requirement is mandatory, and which evidence has authority when interpretations conflict.
