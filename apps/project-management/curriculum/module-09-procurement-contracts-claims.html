Chapter 09 · Cost and procurement

Buying the work, and who carries the risk

A contract is not paperwork, it is a risk-allocation machine. This chapter covers how you solicit the work, which contract type puts the cost risk on the owner or the contractor, and what to do when the job changes, slips, or ends up in a fight.

Contract types: where the risk sits

Here is the idea that matters most in this whole chapter. A contract type is a decision about who eats the overrun if the job costs more than planned. A Firm Fixed Price (FFP) puts that risk on the contractor. A Time and Materials (T&M) or cost-plus contract puts it on the owner. A Guaranteed Maximum Price (GMP) splits it. When the job changes, or goes wrong

No job runs exactly to contract. The clean path is a change order: both sides agree on the change, the price, and the time, and sign. The messy path is a claim: the contractor asserts they are owed more and you do not agree yet. The difference is agreement.

Contracts also carry a stick for lateness. Liquidated damages (LDs) are a set dollar amount owed per day late, agreed up front so nobody has to prove the actual loss. Work one out.

When a claim will not settle, resolution climbs a ladder, and every rung costs more money, more time, and more of the relationship. Click up it.

Leader's call

Two moves prevent most contract pain. Match the contract type to who should own the risk (fixed price when the design is done, shared or cost-reimbursable when it is not), and settle changes early, at the bottom of the ladder. A change order signed this month is a fraction of the cost of a claim litigated next year.

What to carry out of this chapter

  • 1.
    Match the solicitation to the buy. IFB for a finished design (low bid), RFP when approach matters (best value), RFQ for qualifications-based hiring like engineers.
  • 2.
    The contract type allocates the cost risk. Fixed price puts it on the contractor, cost-plus on the owner, GMP shares it. Choose on purpose.
  • 3.
    Administration runs on instruments. Submittals, pay applications, retainage, and lien waivers control quality, money, and legal exposure.
  • 4.
    Settle low on the ladder. Agreed change orders beat claims; negotiation beats litigation, every time, on cost and on relationship.

The leader's bottom line

A contract is where you decide who carries the risk, so put the risk on the party best able to control it, and then manage changes so small disagreements never grow into claims. Do those two things and procurement stops being where projects go to bleed.

Questions people actually ask

Chapter 09 FAQ

Is lowest bid always the required choice?

No. The answer depends on the applicable procurement method and law. Sealed bidding emphasizes price after responsiveness and responsibility, while negotiated best-value methods can weigh stated nonprice factors.

Does fixed price remove all owner risk?

No. The owner still carries risks it retains, changes it directs, information it warrants, and events allocated by the contract.

When does a change become a claim?

A change becomes a claim when entitlement, time, or money is disputed under the contract process. Early notice and complete records help resolve it sooner.

Why does contract administration matter after award?

The contract only controls work when submittals, notices, payment evidence, changes, acceptance, and authority are managed consistently.

Can the project manager promise extra work in the field?

Only within delegated authority and the contract procedure. An informal promise can create exposure without an approved scope, price, schedule, or funding record.
