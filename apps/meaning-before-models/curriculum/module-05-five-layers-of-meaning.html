Five Layers of Meaning

A pressure event can be easy to detect and hard to explain. The records exist, but the words customer, active, affected, and current do not mean the same thing in every utility system. You will separate the five jobs that turn those records into controlled artificial intelligence context.

What to remember

A data model structures information. A taxonomy classifies terms. An ontology defines shared concepts and relationships. A semantic layer connects that meaning to governed enterprise data. AI context packages what the task needs now. A context engine assembles the package. A human remains accountable for the utility decision.

Questions learners usually ask

Where these terms still get mixed together

Is a semantic layer the same thing as an ontology?

No. The ontology defines shared meaning. The semantic layer resolves that meaning to governed records, metrics, and services. For example, the ontology defines ActiveAccount. The semantic layer maps that term to CIS status values and effective dates.

Is a taxonomy enough?

A taxonomy can classify customers as residential, commercial, or critical facility. The exposure question also needs relationships: an account serves a premise, the premise is in a zone, and the event affects the zone.

Is a prompt the same thing as AI context?

A prompt may carry part of the context. The governed task package also includes resolved identity, evidence, definitions, effective policy, time, permissions, workflow state, known conflicts, and output limits.

Does an ontology replace our source data models?

No. CIS, GIS, SCADA, laboratory, and work-management systems still need models suited to their jobs. The ontology gives those systems shared terms and relationships where reuse matters.

Does good context make a generative model deterministic?

No. Good context can make the evidence path more stable and reduce ambiguity. Generated wording may still vary because model and decoding behavior remain probabilistic.

Can a knowledge graph guarantee the answer is true?

No. A graph can make identities, relationships, sources, and rules explicit. A stale GIS relationship can still be wrong. Provenance, validation, review, and human authority remain necessary.

When is an RDF graph unnecessary?

If one governed table answers a bounded question and the meaning does not need to cross systems, teams, or applications, use the simpler design. Graphs earn their place when identity and relationships are central.

What supports this lesson

The five-job distinction is an OWOS teaching framework assembled from W3C standards, mapping and federation documentation, and Hardeep Anand's approved course direction. Vendor terms vary. The Zone 3 scenario is instructional. It does not establish an operating procedure, customer-notification rule, cybersecurity approval, or authority to issue an advisory.
