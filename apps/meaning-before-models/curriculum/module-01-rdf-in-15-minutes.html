RDF in 15 Minutes

You already understand the basic idea behind Resource Description Framework. You use it every time you say that one thing has a particular relationship to another thing. This short lesson turns that ordinary habit into a machine-readable statement.

1
Start with a question

What relationship would help at 2:00 a.m.?

A pump alarm appears in the control room. The screen says Pump P-104. An operator does not need another pile of data. The operator needs to know what that pump serves, where it is, what work is open, and which customers could be affected.

Here are three ordinary questions. In water: which pressure zone does this pump serve? In wastewater: which lift station discharges to this force main? In stormwater: which outfall drains this catchment? Each question asks for the same basic unit of understanding: how one thing relates to another thing.

Your job is to name two things and the relationship between them. Nothing more technical is required yet.

Your job is to preserve direction, stable identifiers, source authority, and the boundary between a resource and a recorded value.

Your job is to ask whether the relationship is reusable, owned, and important enough to govern across teams.

Before you answer

Choose the statement that could be reused by operations, maintenance, planning, and an artificial intelligence application without guessing. Then read the scene from the alarm to the affected zone. Notice that a label on a screen is not yet a relationship.

2
Build it

Subject, predicate, object

The World Wide Web Consortium calls this three-part statement a triple. The thing you are talking about is the subject. The named relationship is the predicate. The thing or value on the other end is the object.

So the ordinary sentence “Pump P-104 serves Pressure Zone 3” becomes: Pump_P104 → serves → Pressure_Zone_3. The arrows matter. If you reverse the subject and object without changing the predicate, you say that the pressure zone serves the pump. The words are still recognizable, but the meaning is wrong.

Resource Description Framework (RDF) gives this pattern a standard form. A resource can be an asset, place, person, event, procedure, or document. A literal can be a number, date, or text value.

Use the bench

3
Connect it

Three triples become a path

One triple is useful. A chain of triples is where the larger idea becomes visible. Add “Pressure Zone 3 contains Premise 88” and “Premise 88 serves Account 901.” A machine can now follow a path from the pump to the service account.

This connected set of identified things and named relationships is a graph. The circles-and-lines picture is only a visualization. The graph itself is the machine-readable statements underneath it.

Take it to work

Write one relationship your utility needs

Do not start by trying to model the enterprise. Start with a relationship that answers one recurring question. Name the subject, predicate, object, source, and question it supports. That is enough for a useful first conversation with operations, GIS, maintenance, customer service, or data staff.

Questions people actually ask

RDF primer FAQ

Is RDF a database?

No. RDF is a data model for expressing statements as triples. You can store those triples in a graph database, generate them virtually from tables, exchange them as files, or expose them through an application interface. A wastewater utility could keep work orders in its existing enterprise asset management system and map selected relationships into RDF without replacing that system.

Is the graph the circles-and-lines picture?

The picture is one view of the graph. The graph is the underlying set of identified resources and directed relationships. A large utility graph may contain millions of statements, even though a screen shows only the path needed for the current question.

Why not put the same information in a spreadsheet?

You can, and for one small local task a spreadsheet may be the better choice. RDF becomes valuable when the same pump, zone, premise, account, procedure, and permit need to connect across systems and be queried through reusable relationships.

Can the object be a number or date?

Yes. “Pump P-104 has design flow 2,500 gallons per minute” can use a typed numeric literal as the object. The lesson used another resource as the object because a pressure zone has its own identity and relationships.

Does writing a triple prove the statement is true?

No. The triple makes the claim explicit. Trust still depends on the source, mapping, effective time, reviewer, and authority. Later modules add identity controls, provenance, validation, and inference boundaries.

Evidence boundary

Pump P-104, Pressure Zone 3, Premise 88, and Account 901 are instructional examples. RDF definitions are grounded in World Wide Web Consortium standards. The example does not establish an operational fact or authorize an action.
