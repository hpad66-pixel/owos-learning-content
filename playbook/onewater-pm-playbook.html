SET

Before you start: model, effort & the project folder

Two dials

Model. Pick Claude Fable 5 for the thinking that decides outcomes. Effort. High is home base for Parts 1 and 2. Extra high and Max mostly cost time and tokens without a better answer. Drop to Medium or Low for routine drafting — Fable runs roughly twice the price of a mid-tier model, so run grunt work cheaper and bring it in for the decisions (Sheet 16).

One house style, reused everywhere

Every visual — boards, dashboards, cost reports, Gantt charts — is an HTML file you open in any browser. Pin one style line so the whole set looks like One Water OS, not a pile of screenshots.

Prompt · house styleask Droobi ▸
Style for anything you build: One Water OS "Clearwater" — Inter type, water
blue (#0A78BA) as the system color, one gold accent (#A97B0F), calm light
surfaces, tabular numbers for anything financial. Internal dashboards may go
dark; public pages stay light. No clip art.

Droobi's one rule

Every project is a folder of plain files — the brief, notes, Board.md, Budget.csv, Risks.md, Permits.md. The pretty HTML is always regenerated from those files; never the only copy of the truth. Stay model-agnostic on purpose: if any model is down, paste the same files into another and keep moving.

00

Classify before you publish

The idea

Later sheets publish a public site in seconds by dragging a folder to the web. Before that's safe, every file gets a classification and a hard line decides what may leave the control center. In water this isn't bureaucracy — network schematics, SCADA detail, valve keys, critical-facility locations, and security plans can be protected critical infrastructure information, a public-safety matter, not a preference.

Hard rule

Infrastructure detail never goes on a public URL. Not system maps, not asset IDs, not security information, not the risk register, not vendor pricing. When in doubt, it's Restricted. The public site gets schedule, plain-language status, and contact info — nothing an adversary or competitor could use.

PUBLIC INTERNAL RESTRICTED CRITICAL / CEII

Public — anyone (schedule, notices). Internal — team + client (budgets, submittals). Restricted — need-to-know (pricing, risk register, easements). Critical — controlled infrastructure data, never in a shared drop.

Prompt · classification passask Droobi ▸
Read every file in the [PROJECT] folder. Tag each Public, Internal,
Restricted, or Critical/CEII, with a one-line why. Default anything that
looks like infrastructure, security, or critical-facility detail to
Restricted-or-higher. Then list exactly which files are cleared for the
public site. Save as Classification.md — that list is the only thing allowed
into /public.
01Set it up

Goal, then constraints, then context. Get these right and everything downstream sharpens.

01

Give it the goal, not the plan

The idea

The habit to break: spelling out every step — "first the locates, then the MOT plan, then order pipe." With Fable that detail makes the output worse. You're hiring a senior PM, not programming a form-filler. Give it the goal and let it build the plan; its plan is usually better, because it sees moves you can't — like the fact that a permit condition, not the pipe delivery, threatens your date.

Why it matters

Your step list is the weakest part of your prompt. It locks in your assumptions and hides the insight you're paying for. Ask for the decision and the justification, not the internal monologue.

Millpond — the running job

Throughout: the Millpond 12″ water-main replacement — 1.2 miles along a county road, one creek crossing, live tie-ins to an in-service system, and a gas main being replaced in the same corridor next quarter. Small enough to hold in your head, complex enough to break in every interesting way.

Prompt · goal over planask Droobi ▸
I need to [goal in one sentence] for [PROJECT]. Delivery:
[in-house / design-bid-build / CMAR / consulting]. My experience with this
kind of work: [honest]. Build the plan you'd want if you were accountable
for the result — permits, locates, procurement, tie-ins included — and tell
me where my assumptions are wrong.
Prompt · plan on a pageask Droobi ▸
Now give me that plan as a single-page visual: the goal, the phases, the key
milestones with dates, and the top three risks. One screen, glanceable, as an
HTML page I can reopen. [house style line]
02

Give it the constraints

The idea

Constraints aren't limitations you apologize for — they're the fuel that makes the plan good. Each real one removes a class of wrong answers. "Under budget X" kills the plans that lose money. "The county has veto over the MOT plan" kills the plans that ignore your approval chain. "Done means pressure-tested, disinfected, cleared by two passing bac-t samples, and accepted — not backfilled" kills the false comfort that sinks jobs at closeout.

The water constraint set — say all of these

  • Money — budget, funding source (capital vs O&M, SRF loan, grant, rate-funded), contingency, what a change order must clear.
  • Hard dates — the fixed one, and what makes it fixed (grant expiry, DOT moratorium, wet-season cutoff, in-service need).
  • Approvals — who vetoes: the utility, county/DOT, environmental agency, easement owners, the client.
  • Regulatory — permits required, and the acceptance standard that means the work is truly done.
  • Fixed vs negotiable — alignment, method, outage windows, working hours.
Prompt · constraints as fuelask Droobi ▸
Here's the goal for [PROJECT]. Constraints: budget [$ + funding source],
hard date [date + why fixed], approvals [who vetoes what], permits
[known set], fixed vs negotiable [list]. "Done" means [acceptance
standard — tested, accepted, as-builts filed]. Rebuild the plan against
these and tell me which single constraint is most likely to break the job.

The schedule you can steer from

Constraints plus a hard date make this the moment for a Gantt — and the Gantt makes dependency thinking visible instead of asserted. On Millpond, this is where you see the environmental permit for the creek crossing and the 26-week valve lead both sit on the critical path, and vendor selection does not.

Prompt · steerable Ganttask Droobi ▸
Turn the plan into a Gantt I can steer from, as an HTML page I can reopen and
update. Work backward from [hard date]. Show what depends on what, put
permit and procurement lead times on the bars as real durations, and
highlight the critical path so I can see what threatens the date. [house style]
03

Own your context

The idea

The one that matters most. Goal and constraints tell Droobi what you want; context tells it everything else — the history, the site conditions, the constraint so obvious you forgot to say it. The biggest unknowns live in someone's head, unstated, because nobody realized they were load-bearing. Don't write it all yourself — let Droobi interview you.

Why it matters

Always-on reasoning makes for a good interviewer — each question builds on your last answer instead of marching through a checklist. Keep the output in one place: a project brief (Millpond Brief.md) in the folder Claude reads every session.

What a good interview drags out on Millpond

Around question ten it asks who controls the corridor during construction, and you realize the county thinks it does and FDOT thinks the state-road segment is theirs — which changes your MOT plan and permit path. You'd never have written that down. Now it's in the brief.

Prompt · the interviewask Droobi ▸
Before you plan anything for [PROJECT], interview me. One question at a
time, each building on my last answer. Dig into: constraints I haven't
stated, site and subsurface conditions, existing utilities and conflicts,
what's been tried before and why it did or didn't work, every stakeholder
and what they actually care about, and the risks that worry me. When you
understand it better than my opening description, save it as [PROJECT] Brief.md.
Prompt · the boardask Droobi ▸
With the brief saved, build a live kanban board as a local HTML file (data in
Board.md, HTML regenerated from it): columns for the phases, first cards from
what you learned, and any unresolved question flagged as an open decision. [house style]

04

Consider different paths

The idea

For most decisions, the plan plus a good Blind Spot Pass is enough. For the few that are big, expensive, and hard to reverse — method, delivery model, alignment — make Droobi think in options before it commits. On Millpond the real fork is the creek crossing: open-cut with a temporary bypass vs horizontal directional bore. One is cheaper and slower to permit; the other is dearer and clears the environmental path in half the time.

Why it matters

Most projects commit to the first workable approach and never see the one twice as good for half the effort. But exploring five options on every small choice is its own trap. Match the depth to the cost of being wrong.

Prompt · light (default)ask Droobi ▸
For [decision] on [PROJECT], weigh a few genuinely different
approaches, then recommend one. Show your pick, the runner-up, and why you
passed on it.
Prompt · heavy (big, irreversible calls only)ask Droobi ▸
[Decision] is the call everything else hangs on. Give me five genuinely
different paths, not variations on one idea. For each: how it works, a real
example on a job like this, an honest cost and schedule estimate, permit and
safety implications, fit with my constraints, and the main watch-out. Do not
rank them yet.

Asked to rank immediately, models collapse toward the safe middle. Asked to lay each out on its own terms first, they surface options you'd never have generated. Then put them side by side — cost, schedule, permit risk, safety, constraint fit — and the trade-offs show themselves.

05

The Blind Spot Pass

The most important sheet here

Once you have a draft plan, before you spend money or commit dates, run a deliberately adversarial read of the whole thing. Not a review for encouragement — a list of what the plan has to survive, structured around four quadrants, because naming them forces Droobi into the corners it would otherwise skip:

  • Known knowns — what you believe that actually checks out.
  • Known unknowns — open questions you've spotted, unanswered too long.
  • Unknown knowns — things your own files already show the plan isn't acting on. Reliably the most valuable quadrant.
  • Unknown unknowns — risks you show no sign of having considered.

This works because Droobi holds your entire project folder at once and cross-references it — the brief, the budget, last year's post-job notes, the utility correspondence. That capability is why the pass catches expensive mistakes instead of producing a reflection exercise. The premortem is the single most reliable insight move in PM; this is the premortem with a model that can actually read everything.

What the pass catches on Millpond

Under Unknown knowns it flags that the geotech in your folder notes rock at the creek bank, and your open-cut estimate assumed soil — a number you already had, that the plan was ignoring. It lands on the board as a risk with an owner, and you re-price the crossing before the bid, not after.

Prompt · the Blind Spot Passask Droobi ▸
Here's the full plan for [PROJECT] and all relevant material: [attach
brief, budget, geotech, permits, utility correspondence, prior job notes].
Do a blind spot pass, organized as Known Knowns, Known Unknowns, Unknown
Knowns (things my own materials show the plan isn't acting on), and Unknown
Unknowns. Be adversarial — this is the list of things the plan has to
survive, not a review for encouragement. Render it as a four-quadrant map on
one screen, and add serious risks to the board with an owner and a next
action. [house style]

Run this on High. It's exactly the high-stakes read that setting is built for. The quadrant map matters more than it looks: findings placed in the four quadrants teach the framework a list never does — and the empty quadrant is its own message.

The risk register

The idea

The Blind Spot Pass finds risks. The register tracks them — over the months where a risk quietly moves from "watching" to "it happened." Each gets a probability, an impact, an owner, an observable trigger, and two distinct responses: mitigation (lower the odds now) and contingency (what you do if it fires anyway). A risk without an owner and a trigger is a worry, not a managed risk.

07

The critical path & what actually controls it

The idea

On a water job the critical path is almost never the construction. It's the gates in front of it: the permit that takes eight weeks to condition, the locate ticket with a legal clock, the fitting on a half-year lead, the crew that needs the corridor first. Droobi builds the schedule backward from the hard date and puts these controlling items on the bars as real durations — so the plan competes with reality, not a wish.

1 · Permits & agency approvals

County / municipal ROW, FDOT utility and road-crossing / MOT approvals, environmental (creek crossing, dewatering), and the utility's regulatory acceptance. Each has a lead time and conditions that can reshape the work. Tracked in Permits.md.

2 · Locates — Sunshine 811 and the legal clock

In Florida every dig starts with a Sunshine 811 ticket: two full business days' notice, a positive-response check that every operator has actually marked, and a ticket life that expires and must be refreshed. "We called it in" is not a locate; a positive response with marks in the ground is. Miss the refresh and the crew stands down. This belongs on the schedule as a recurring, expiring item. The uniform marking code, so field and plan speak one language:

RED · ELECTRIC YELLOW · GAS/OIL ORANGE · COMM BLUE · WATER GREEN · SEWER PINK · SURVEY WHITE · PROPOSED DIG

3 · Procurement & long-lead items

Large valves, ductile-iron pipe and fittings, restraint, castings, electrical gear — leads run from weeks to well over half a year. Release long-leads at notice-to-proceed, not when the crew reaches them. Droobi flags every item whose lead is longer than the time until it's needed, and gives the last responsible order date.

4 · Coordination with other projects

The corridor is shared. The parallel gas replacement, a paving overlay, a franchise-utility relocation — any can lock your alignment or hand you a joint-trench opportunity that halves restoration cost. Exactly what an agent reading across project folders is for (Sheet 15).

5 · Weather, outage & moratorium windows

Wet-season limits, DOT paving moratoria, planned outage windows for tie-ins, permitted work hours — hard gates the schedule is sequenced around, not through.

08

Safety & compliance

The idea

Safety isn't bolted on at the end — it's a schedule and cost driver, and on water work it can stop the job cold. Trench protection over five feet, a competent person on site, confined-space entry for vaults and wet wells, traffic control that matches the approved MOT, dewatering per permit. Droobi builds a compliance checklist tied to the phases, so the requirement shows up before the crew hits the condition, not in the incident report after.

  • Trench & excavation — protective system over 5 ft, competent-person inspections, spoil setback; Florida Trench Safety Act line item where required.
  • Confined space — entry permits, atmospheric testing, attendant, rescue plan for vaults, wet wells, manholes.
  • Traffic / MOT — approved plan matching the permit, in place before lane work.
  • Environmental — erosion & sediment control, dewatering discharge, creek-crossing conditions, spill response.
  • Acceptance / regulatory — pressure test, disinfection, two consecutive passing bac-t samples, as-builts, and the sign-off that means done.
Prompt · safety & compliance checklistask Droobi ▸
Build a safety & compliance checklist for [PROJECT], tied to the phases,
saved as Compliance.md with an HTML view. Cover excavation/trench
protection, confined space, traffic/MOT, environmental controls, and the
acceptance standard (testing, disinfection, clearance, as-builts). For each:
which phase triggers it, who's responsible, the evidence that proves it was
done, and the regulatory reference. Flag anything that must be in place
before a phase can start. [house style]
09

Stakeholders & who approves what

The idea

Water work has more veto-holders than most projects — the utility, the county, DOT, an environmental agency, easement and property owners, franchise utilities in the corridor, the client, and the public. A one-line "public vs internal" split doesn't capture decision rights. A simple RACI does: for each decision, who is Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, Informed. Get the "A" wrong on the MOT plan or the vendor list and you find out at the worst moment.

Role lenses

Target every report to the reader. One Water OS reads five lenses — utility manager (decision risk, ownership), operator (practical, field), researcher (evidence gaps), consultant (patterns, scopes), data leader (governance, readiness). Name the lens and the report writes itself in their language.

Prompt · stakeholder map & RACIask Droobi ▸
Build a stakeholder map and RACI for [PROJECT] from the brief. List every
party — utility, county, DOT, environmental agency, easement owners,
franchise utilities, client, public — what each cares about, and their veto
power. Then a RACI table for the key decisions and deliverables (permits,
MOT plan, alignment, vendor list, tie-in timing, acceptance). Flag any
decision where accountability is unclear or contested. Save as Stakeholders.md. [house style]

10

Phases, checkpoints & honest status

The idea

Break the job into phases with a hard checkpoint at each boundary — not because Droobi can't handle the whole thing, but because you need forced moments where reality gets compared to plan before more money moves. At each checkpoint define three things: what must be true to proceed, what evidence proves it (a signed permit, a passing test, a real quantity — not a feeling), and the fallback.

The grounding rule — steal this if nothing else

Every progress claim must point to a specific artifact: a positive response, a signed permit, a passing bac-t result, a number from the pay estimate. If a claim has no artifact behind it, it goes in a separate "believed but unverified" list. Never blend the two. This nearly eliminates fabricated status — and it works on your human crew's updates just as well.

"On track" is the most dangerous phrase in project management, because most of the time nobody has checked. A checkpoint with required evidence turns a feeling into a fact.

Prompt · phase gates & groundingask Droobi ▸
Structure [PROJECT] as phases with a hard checkpoint at each boundary.
For each: what must be true to proceed, what evidence proves it (a document,
a test result, a positive response, a number — not a feeling), and the
fallback. Separately, for all future status: every progress claim must cite
a specific artifact, and anything without one goes in a "believed but
unverified" list. Never blend the two. Reflect the phases as columns on the board.
Prompt · status dashboardask Droobi ▸
Build the status dashboard for the control center as an HTML page: each

11

The control center & the public site

Two screens, one clean line between them

The control center is private. The board, status dashboard, schedule, cost tracker, risk register, permit tracker — local HTML files in the project folder, for your team only. Data lives in plain files; HTML is regenerated from them.

The public site is for everyone else — a small /public folder with the schedule, milestone status in plain language, a contact page, community notices. What never goes there is set by Sheet 00: no budget, no negotiations, no risk register, no infrastructure detail.

From board to real software

The board starts as a static HTML file. It becomes real software when you connect it to the folder: most browsers can read the markdown files in a folder as a simple database, so Droobi builds a working control center with no server and no stack. Routine updates then happen by moving a card, not by burning a prompt.

Prompt · the control centerask Droobi ▸
Turn this into a control center for [PROJECT], real software. It opens in
the browser, connects to the project folder once, and shows a clear
connected / not-connected indicator plus a "last saved" confirmation on
every write. Build in: a working kanban (drag cards, add cards, check items
off, saved straight to Board.md), a quick-capture box that appends whatever
I type to Inbox.md with a timestamp, and a sidebar listing every file so I
can open the brief, budget, or trackers without leaving the page. One strict
rule: the portal may only write to Board.md and Inbox.md. Every other file is
read-only; changes to them happen through you in conversation, so the
reasoning gets recorded.

That last rule is the point. The portal touches only Board.md and Inbox.md; everything else changes through conversation, so the reasoning behind each change gets recorded — which, in a regulated environment, is your decision audit trail.

Prompt · the public siteask Droobi ▸
Build a /public folder as a small static site for [PROJECT], using ONLY

language, a contact/notice page, community FAQ. Nothing internal — no budget,

Publish it as a static site, re-publish after each weekly ritual, and point one permanent URL at the current address so stakeholders memorize one door and you swap what's behind it. Rehearse that update loop once before you depend on it.

13

Reports on demand

The idea

Because the truth lives in plain files and every claim ties to an artifact, any report is a read away — no re-keying, no stitching spreadsheets the night before the meeting. You keep the files current; Droobi assembles the audience-specific view. The same source produces a two-line exec update and a twelve-page funder package without either drifting from the other.

  • Weekly status report — RAG by workstream, milestones hit/slipped, look-ahead, each claim with evidence.
  • Executive one-pager — done / at-risk / need-from-you, in the reader's language.
  • Cost report — Sheet 12, for client or funder.
  • Safety report — inspections, findings, corrective actions, any incident.
  • Permit & locate status — approved, pending, expiring.
  • Stakeholder / community update — public-cleared, plain language.
Prompt · any report, one patternask Droobi ▸
Generate a [status / executive / safety / permit / stakeholder] report for
[PROJECT] as of [date], reading the whole folder. Ground every claim in a
specific artifact; put anything unverified in a separate section. Write it
for [audience / role lens] in their language, not mine. Keep it to [length],
and end with the decisions or actions I need from the reader.

Don't just track the work — make Droobi do it

The trap with a control center this good is treating Droobi as the thing that keeps track while you do all the doing. It's agentic: given access, it can draft permit cover letters, assemble the submittal log, chase the RFI list, prep the pay estimate — not just note they exist. Every time the board grows, ask what of it Droobi can own, and what it needs from you to do the rest.

14

The weekly ritual

The idea

A job that runs six months can't live in one conversation — sessions end and context gets summarized. The fix is a rhythm, not a memory trick. Between sessions you jot what happened into the notes file, in plain language, dated. Once a week (or every few days when things heat up) you run one working session that reads everything, reconciles it, and regenerates every screen. Two standing habits: keep the brief current, and convert relative dates to absolute ones — "next Friday" is poison in a file reread three weeks later.

Prompt · the weekly runask Droobi ▸
Weekly update for [PROJECT]. Read the brief, the notes, and everything
that changed in the folder this week. First, tell me the current state as you
understand it and anything that looks stale or contradictory. Then: re-score
the risk register, update the schedule and flag any critical-path slip,
refresh permit and locate status, update the board and cost tracker, tell me
what's newly at risk with the artifact behind each claim, recommend what to
communicate this week and to whom, and regenerate the control-center pages and
the /public site.

What one weekly run catches

Your board has the crew doing tie-ins next week, but the facts — a locate ticket expiring in nine days and a valve that hasn't shipped — say it can't happen. And the "12 lots capped" decision from May quietly became 13 in a June field note. The readback surfaces both before they compound.

Droobi & the cheaper models

The idea

Fable is powerful and not cheap — roughly twice the price of a mid-tier model per unit of work. The skill is knowing when it earns the premium: Fable's advantage grows with task complexity. A quick test — if you could hand the task to a capable assistant with one sentence and not check their work, it's not a Fable task. If being wrong costs real money or weeks, it is.

Across a six-month job, most days run on a cheaper model — drafting the notice-to-residents, formatting the agenda, summarizing a progress meeting — and you bring Fable in for the moments that decide the outcome: the master schedule, the method decision, the Blind Spot Pass, a go/no-go call, the portfolio overlap read. Because your brief and notes are plain text on purpose, if any model is unavailable you paste the same files into another and keep moving.

Prompt · which model, honestlyask Droobi ▸
Here's a task for [PROJECT]: [describe it]. Before you do it, tell me
honestly whether this needs a top-tier model like Fable or whether a cheaper,
faster one would do it just as well. Base it on how much real ambiguity and
judgment the task involves, not on making it fancy.
17

Retrospective & seed the next job

The idea

The job closes out, and right then you're holding the richest material you'll ever have on it — and it evaporates fast. Because the whole folder is still there (brief, notes, board history, budget, risk register, the Blind Spot Pass), Droobi can compare what you planned against what actually happened and tell you where the two diverged and why. Not from memory — from the artifacts. Which risks you flagged came true, which you missed, where the budget drifted, which "done" turned out not to be.

That output isn't a document nobody reopens. It's a head start on the next job of this kind — the patterns that bit you this time, written down before you begin the next.

Prompt · the retrospectiveask Droobi ▸
[PROJECT] is closed out. Read the whole folder and run a retrospective

why, which flagged risks came true and which were missed, where budget or
schedule drifted, what permits or locates caused delay, and the top three
changes for next time. Save as [PROJECT] Retro.md.
