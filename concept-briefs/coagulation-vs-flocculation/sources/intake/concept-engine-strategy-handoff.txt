One hard constraint I had to enforce, and it's worth knowing why. Gold cannot be text on white. Your gold measures 1.60:1 against white — unreadable, not a preference. So the rule is:

On black: gold is text, headlines, numbers, borders. Full strength.
On blue: white heading, cyan label, gold rule.
On white: text is near-black. Labels and links are the deep blue at 7:1. Gold appears only as a physical object — the 56px rule under each lede, the left border on every card, the table's top rule. Never as a letterform.
The result is that gold still reads as the brand constant across all three surfaces, because it's always doing something structural. It just stops being asked to be legible where it can't be.

The honest read: the brief is not the product. The graph of briefs is. One brief competes with a free AWWA handout. Three hundred interlinked briefs with tiered evidence, feeding courses and competency records, is something nobody can assemble quickly.

2. Vendors and consultants — three tiers, ascending in both price and integrity risk.

Directory listing (cheap, safe) — topic-matched presence on relevant briefs. What the rail demonstrates.
Sponsored authorship (mid) — a firm's PE co-authors a brief, named, with their credential attached. They get authority; you get review capacity, which is your actual bottleneck.
Private tenant briefs (expensive) — a utility or firm licenses the engine to produce internal briefs on their own SOPs, inside their own tenant. This is where the real money is, and it has zero editorial risk because it never touches public content.
3. The advertising question, straight. You said no shame, and you're right — but there is one thing that will kill this, and it isn't taste. Your entire differentiator is the evidence tier system. The moment a reader suspects a CONTESTED verdict was influenced by a coagulant supplier, every brief in the graph loses its value at once. Not gradually. At once.

So the rail ships with an editorial firewall stated in the document itself: partners matched by topic not payment, no review rights, no influence on tiers, no ability to remove a correction, no sponsored claims. That disclosure is not a legal formality — it's the thing that lets you charge a premium, because it makes the sponsorship safe to see. Publishers who hide it get less money, not more.

Rate card logic: price on brief traffic × topic specificity, not impressions. A coagulant supplier on the coagulation brief is worth ten times a generic banner because the reader is mid-decision.
