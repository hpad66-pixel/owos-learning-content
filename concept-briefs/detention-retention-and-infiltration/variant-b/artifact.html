Detention, retention, and infiltration — and why the name on the drawing is the weakest evidence you have.

Who this is for

Anyone who has to talk about, pay for, inspect, approve, explain, or answer for a stormwater asset without being the person who designed it.

What you need first

Only that rain runs off streets and roofs into drains. No hydrology, no hydraulics, no regulatory background.

Why it matters

Utilities approve assets that do not do what their name implies, and add capacity when the real problem was a blocked outlet. Neither mistake requires carelessness. Both require trusting a word.

How long

About 15 minutes. Nothing here requires you to type anything.

  1. Say what detention, retention, and infiltration actually describe, and why the three words are used inconsistently in practice.
  2. Trace where water can go from any storage practice, including the routes nobody intended.
  3. Read a hydrograph well enough to know when someone is overstating what it proves.
  4. Explain why a lower peak is not less water.
  5. Work out how a maintenance condition changes the route without diagnosing a real asset.

In 30 seconds

If you stop reading here, keep these four.

  1. Detention holds water and lets it out later. It changes when water arrives far more than how much.
  2. Retention means two different things depending on who is speaking. Ask which.
  3. Infiltration means water crossed into the ground. That is a doorway, not a destination.
  4. A name describes a category. Only the route describes behaviour.

The problem

Everything checked out. It flooded anyway.

A residential street floods during a storm. Upstream sits a stormwater pond that was built for that development. The utility sends an inspector.

Here is what the inspection found.

  • PASSThe pond held water during the storm and returned to its normal level afterward.
  • PASSThe outlet structure was clear. No blockage, no debris.
  • PASSThe embankment was sound. No erosion, no settlement.
  • PASSRecords confirm the pond was built to the approved plan and accepted by the utility.
  • ANDThe street flooded.

Nothing on that list is wrong. So what would you ask next?

Most people ask whether the pond is big enough. That is a reasonable guess and it is usually the wrong first question, because it assumes the pond was supposed to prevent this. Work out what the pond actually does first. Then the flooding either follows from it or it does not.

The words you need

Six words, each with what it does not tell you.

That last part is the useful half. Almost every expensive misunderstanding in this subject comes from a word being asked to prove more than it can.

Detention

Holding water temporarily and letting it out afterward.

Picture it: a crowd leaving a stadium through one gate. Everyone gets out. It just takes longer, and the street outside is never as crowded as the stadium.

What it does not tell you: how much water leaves in total. Almost all of it does. Detention is a delay device, not a disposal device.

Retention

In the strict sense, water kept back so it does not leave later through a surface outlet. In everyday use, "retention pond" usually just means a pond with water in it all the time.

Picture it: a neighbourhood pond that always looks full.

What it does not tell you: which of those two meanings the speaker intends. This is the single most reliable source of confusion in the subject.

Infiltration

Water crossing from the surface down into soil or engineered material.

Picture it: rain vanishing into a lawn while the driveway beside it runs.

What it does not tell you: where the water finally goes. It may reach groundwater, move sideways, be taken up by plants, or land in a pipe and come straight back out downstream.

Permanent pool

The water a wet pond is designed to keep between storms.

Picture it: the normal water line you see on an ordinary dry day, with a ring of vegetation around it.

What it does not tell you: that the pond is full. Storm storage is normally provided above the permanent pool, so a pond that looks full may have all of its working capacity still available.

Outlet and overflow

The outlet controls how water leaves under ordinary conditions. The overflow is the route water uses when it rises higher than the outlet can handle.

Picture it: a concrete riser with a small hole near its base, and a broad low notch in the bank that is usually dry and mowed.

What it does not tell you: that either one is clear, or that the overflow has ever been tested by a real storm.

Tailwater

The water level in the receiving system just downstream of an outlet.

Picture it: the creek the pond drains into, already running high from the same storm.

What it does not tell you: anything you can see from the pond. And it matters, because an outlet does not discharge into empty air. It discharges into whatever is already there.

The measurement everyone misreads

A lower peak is not less water.

This is the single most common overstatement in stormwater conversation, and it is easy to see once someone shades the area instead of just saying it. Press play.

Back to the street

Now the inspection makes sense.

Every item on that inspection list was true, and none of them was evidence about flooding. The inspection confirmed that the pond was in good condition. The complaint was about capacity and timing somewhere downstream. Those are different claims needing different evidence.

Three explanations now fit the facts without anything being broken:

The pond was never meant to prevent this. Detention lowers and delays a discharge peak. It does not remove water. If the street floods because the total volume arriving downstream exceeds what the drainage there can carry, a working pond upstream changes the timing and not the outcome.

The creek was already high. Tailwater reduces the difference in level across the outlet, so the pond releases more slowly than expected and holds water longer. The pond looks like it is misbehaving. It is responding to a downstream condition nobody inspected.

The delay landed at the wrong moment. EPA's stormwater design guide reports that some basins "actually increase downstream peak discharges as a result of the overlapping of their detained volumes with mainstream peaks," and that when volumes from different tributaries join downstream "there is nothing to prevent them from combining to produce inadvertently high peak rates." A basin can satisfy its own release condition at its own outlet and still make things worse at a point further down.

EPA also notes the other side: selectively located basins do reduce flood peaks. The conclusion is not that detention fails. It is that the outcome depends on placement and timing across a watershed, and cannot be read off a single site.

Which is the same sentence you started with. A name describes a category. Only the route describes behaviour.

The same rain, the wrong pipe

Everything so far assumed the water stayed in the stormwater system.

It does not always. Rain that reaches a separate sanitary sewer becomes someone else's problem and someone else's cost, and the utility pays to carry and treat water that was never supposed to be there. This is why the route matters beyond stormwater.

Intended

Into the stormwater system

Runoff reaches an inlet, moves through storage, leaves by an outlet or an overflow, and arrives at a receiving water.

Everything on this page so far describes this route.

Unintended

Into the sanitary sewer

Federal regulation names both routes. Infiltration is water entering "from the ground through such means as defective pipes, pipe joints, connections, or manholes." Inflow is water entering from "roof leaders, cellar drains, yard drains, area drains, manhole covers, cross connections between storm sewers and sanitary sewers."

That water is conveyed, pumped, and treated at the utility's expense, and it consumes capacity that was sized for wastewater.

The trap is thinking you can tell them apart from a flow chart. The regulation separates the two cleanly, and it separates them by route, not by timing. The flow record does not carry the route.

The tempting shortcut says a sharp response that ends with the storm means inflow, and a slow response that decays over days means infiltration. EPA's own research reporting removes the second half of that. Rainfall-derived infiltration typically continues past the end of rainfall, and then: a system "may experience a fast RDI response, a slow RDI response, or both." Infiltration is not confined to the slow signature, so a fast response does not prove inflow.

Watch how EPA handles the quantity in practice. Its stormwater model reference treats rainfall-derived inflow and infiltration as one combined term, and says estimates of it "are almost always derived from actual wastewater flow records" rather than by modelling the processes that produced it. The rainfall driven portion gets measured as an aggregate, not resolved into its two mechanisms.

So the shape narrows the question. It does not answer it. Sealing pipe defects does nothing about a roof leader plumbed into the wrong pipe, and disconnecting downspouts does nothing about a cracked lateral.

Before you leave

Carry three things forward.

What changed

You stopped reading a pond by its name and started reading it by where the water can go.

What to observe

The inlet, the normal water level, what sits above it, the outlet, the overflow, and what the receiving water was doing at the same time.

What not to assume

That a passed inspection is evidence about flooding, that a lower peak is less water, or that a working asset means a solved problem.
