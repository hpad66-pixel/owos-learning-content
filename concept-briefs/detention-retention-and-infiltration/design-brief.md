# Concept Brief Design Brief

## Design decision

The pre-research design is retired. It concentrated on three routes and two technical boundaries,
but it did not carry the approved white paper's complete teaching argument. The rebuilt experience
must explain the familiar assets, show water through time and below ground, connect condition to
performance, connect stormwater to wastewater I&I, and make permit and financial value visible.

The public Concept Brief remains a United States national concept product using federal and EPA
authority. Florida provides a familiar visual lens and has a separately governed private research
paper. No Florida requirement, regional criterion, or state definition enters the public brief as
universal authority.

Owner review of preview 0.2 found that the page introduced terminology too late, provided too little
instructor explanation, used weak editorial gutters, and did not demonstrate sufficient graphic-text
alignment. Preview 0.3 therefore begins with visible definitions, introduces every later topic before
its graphic, increases the editorial gutter, and places diagrams inside consistent aligned frames.

## Intake and evidence

- Source types: Current federal statute and regulation, current EPA guidance, USGS science, exact
  portfolio direction, approved national white paper, and a separate private Florida research
  companion.
- Public source visibility: Public federal sources and approved owner direction.
- Private jurisdictional source visibility: Florida sources remain under `research/` and are
  prohibited from public national claims, citations, Graph evidence, and assessments.
- Evidence cutoff: July 26, 2026.
- Known limitations: Terminology varies. Performance depends on site, design, construction, storm,
  downstream condition, operation, and maintenance. A conceptual illustration cannot establish
  design adequacy, infiltration suitability, flood protection, permit compliance, or condition.
- Claims requiring qualified review: Detention and wet-pond mechanisms, infiltration and
  groundwater interaction, hydrograph interpretation, maintenance consequences, I&I effects,
  permit framing, and every technical statement carried into a visual or assessment.
- Verification state: Sources and claims are inventoried. Author source tracing and reviewer
  dossier preparation may proceed. Independent verification and qualified practitioner reviews
  remain hard gates.

## Learner job

- Primary question: What happened to the water, what controlled it, what result was intended, and
  what evidence shows the complete system still works?
- Misconception or risk: A familiar label, a permanent pond, a dry basin, or a green-looking
  practice proves the pathway, purpose, condition, or performance.
- Intended audience: Stormwater and wastewater professionals, inspectors, engineers, asset
  managers, finance professionals, executives, elected officials, regulators, developers,
  educators, and interested community members.
- Decision or action affected: How a learner describes an asset, reads a diagram, interprets a
  hydrograph, asks a design or maintenance question, reviews a budget request, explains a permit,
  or connects rainfall to wastewater I&I.
- Transfer action: Answer the 11-question conversation tool for an unfamiliar stormwater system and
  add the closing statement, producing a 12-field pathway, responsibility, evidence, and value work
  product.
- What this brief will not attempt: Design, size, model, certify, permit, inspect, operate, repair,
  or determine compliance for a real system.

## Design fingerprint

- Graphite identity treatment or approved exception: Standard Graphite identity and shared public
  presentation shell. No exception requested.
- Opening pattern: A short foundation defines detention, retention, infiltration, stormwater runoff,
  storage, outlet, outfall, overflow, bypass, permanent pool, and underdrain before one storm appears
  over a street, dry basin, wet pond, permeable surface, and sanitary sewer.
- Narrative archetype: One-storm systems investigation.
- Central mental model: Follow the water, identify the control, and name the responsibility.
- Signature mechanism: A deterministic storm timeline that can be paused at before, rising,
  peak, recession, and recovery states. At each state, the learner can reveal surface storage,
  controlled release, permanent pool, subsurface movement, overflow, tailwater, and unintended I&I.
- Dominant visual: A wide system cutaway that moves from rainfall and streets through dry and wet
  storage, subsurface practices, outlet, outfall, receiving water, and a separate sanitary sewer.
- Interaction signature: Trace, pause, question, and prove. The learner traces the route, pauses
  time, selects the controlling feature, and chooses the evidence needed to support a claim.
- Role treatment: The same event is read through water, environmental, asset, financial, and
  public-accountability ledgers. Roles do not get separate decorative cards. They receive different
  questions applied to the same system evidence.
- Closing action: Complete the 11-question conversation tool for an unfamiliar system, then state
  one result, one uncertainty, one responsibility, and one next piece of evidence as the twelfth
  field.
- Surface rhythm: Graphite storm opening, pale route map, blue time chamber, ivory permanent-pool
  explanation, dark subsurface cutaway, pale performance lab, blue failure trace, split-system I&I
  scene, ivory permit and value chain, dark role meeting, white applied close.
- Patterns deliberately avoided: An unexplained glossary wall, generic icon grid,
  dashboard of unsupported percentages, universal thresholds, pond identification by appearance,
  Florida criteria presented as national rules, ornamental animation, and the rejected narrow
  three-route prototype.
- Differences from adjacent briefs: The Storm Hydrograph will teach the graph in depth. Water
  Balance will teach accounting in depth. Stormwater Control Measure Maintenance will teach
  inspection and work planning. I&I will teach collection-system investigation. This brief shows
  how those concepts connect around one storm without replacing them.

## Teaching thesis

The brief begins by introducing the subject and defining its three central terms in ordinary
language. It then defines the few system words needed to read the diagrams. Only after that
foundation does rain fall and move through the connected system. Some water waits. Some leaves
later. Some crosses into soil. Some remains in a permanent pool. Some reaches an underdrain or
receiving water. Some enters a sanitary sewer where it does not belong.

This sequence produces three teaching gains:

1. A term is introduced in plain English and then attached to a visible mechanism.
2. A mechanism is connected to an intended result and failure path.
3. A result is connected to evidence, responsibility, and value.

## Experience architecture

### Act 0: Orient before the topic

Before any stormwater content, the rendered brief answers what this is about, who it is for, why it
matters, what the learner will be able to do, how long it takes, and what it does not cover. The
learning objectives are rendered from `learning.yaml` rather than living only inside the package, so
the learner sees the same promise the package makes internally.

This act is produced by the compiler from `learner_experience.orientation` and `learning.outcomes`.
It is not authored as narrative blocks, which keeps the orientation consistent across every brief
and prevents it from being quietly dropped during a revision.

### Act 1: Introduce the topic and define the terms

The learner receives visible plain-English definitions of stormwater runoff, storage, outlet,
outfall, overflow, bypass, permanent pool, and underdrain, alongside the three central terms. Each
definition carries a plain meaning, a concrete example, and an explicit statement of what the term
does not establish. That third part is the instructional payload: it is where the brief repairs the
habit of reading a name as evidence. This foundation prevents later diagrams from becoming
unexplained labels.

### Act 2: Follow the water

The learner sees rainfall become runoff and traces the connected surface system. The instructor
introduces drainage area, inlet, conveyance, storage, outlet, overflow, outfall, and receiving
water. The learner is told where to look first and why every route matters.

### Act 3: Read the storage

A dry basin fills and drains through time. A wet pond then shows the permanent pool, normal water
level, temporary storage above it, control structure, overflow, and downstream tailwater. The
learner sees that dry and wet describe between-storm condition while detention describes temporary
storage and later release.

### Act 4: Look below the surface

A cutaway combines bioretention, trench, permeable pavement, native soil, groundwater, underdrain,
and overflow. The learner follows separate water fractions and learns why infiltration is not a
promise of recharge, treatment, or site suitability.

### Act 5: Read time and performance

A qualitative hydrograph links inflow, storage, and delayed outflow. A performance comparison
separates peak, timing, volume, concentration, and total pollutant load. No quantitative result is
presented as a model prediction.

### Act 6: Break the route

The learner introduces sediment, clogging, outlet blockage, erosion, high downstream water, and an
underdrain problem. The system response changes. The instructor separates observation, hypothesis,
diagnosis, and authorized action.

### Act 7: Put the rain in the wrong pipe

A split-system scene shows the intended stormwater route beside unintended infiltration and inflow
into a separate sanitary sewer. The extra wet-weather flow moves through collection, pumping,
treatment, storage, energy, operations, and capital consequences.

### Act 8: Connect permission, ownership, and money

A permit-to-value handoff shows law and public objective, permit and approved plan, constructed
asset, named responsible party, access, inspection, maintenance, funded work, evidence, and
correction. It teaches the value of permits without offering a compliance interpretation.

### Act 9: Hold the meeting

An elected official, engineer, operator, finance professional, wastewater manager, and community
member receive the same storm evidence. The learner uses the 11-question conversation tool to
separate what is known, assumed, and still needed.

## Learner economy and completion

- In 30 seconds orientation: Detention holds water temporarily. Retention must be tied to its source
  and physical arrangement. Infiltration begins below the surface but does not prove the final
  destination.
- Primary navigation controls, maximum four: Start, Trace storm, Graph, Community.
- Public orientation promise: Understand detention, retention, infiltration, permanent pools,
  hydrographs, maintenance, permits, and I&I through one storm.
- Governance metadata removed from learner-facing copy: Claim counts, internal approval machinery,
  reviewer assignments, package states, and Graph edge identifiers.
- Progressive disclosure: Definitions open beside the visual where first needed. Tooltips reinforce
  terms but never replace visible instruction.
- Three-line close, What changed: You replaced a pond label with a complete water, control,
  responsibility, and evidence story.
- Three-line close, What to observe: Inflow, water level, storage, soil route, outlet, overflow,
  downstream condition, wastewater entry, maintenance condition, and records.
- Three-line close, What not to assume: Appearance, a name, a model, or a permit proves present
  performance.
- Comment placement: After the complete learning experience and any approved commercial content,
  immediately before the footer.
- Graph treatment: Compact top control plus same-screen responsive drawer and full Graph route.
- Community treatment: Compact top control plus same-screen responsive drawer and dedicated route.
- Standard operating procedure boundary: Conceptual checklist only. Facility-specific instructions
  require a separately approved authenticated workflow.
- Inactive commercial treatment: Hidden.
- Short disclaimer: This brief explains the concept. Facility decisions still require your
  approved procedures and qualified judgment.
- Positive-feedback prompt: Which part of the storm route became clear for the first time?
- Testimonial consent language: Exact approved comment, member name, role, and organization for this
  brief only.
- Moderator and withdrawal treatment: Separate steward approval is required. Unfeature preserves
  the Community record.
- Public reader-voices placement and evidence label: True end of the experience, labeled learner
  experience rather than technical evidence.
- Administrator metrics: Views, unique viewers, engaged reads, completion reaches, attempts,
  comments, reviewed comments, consented appreciation, and featured comments.

## Interaction and assessment plan

### Dynamic mechanism

The central storm tracer uses authored qualitative states. It does not calculate hydrology,
hydraulics, infiltration, treatment, flood elevation, or compliance. Each state reveals:

- water location;
- storage condition;
- active outlet or overflow;
- subsurface route;
- downstream boundary;
- unintended wastewater entry where applicable; and
- evidence that could support the explanation.

### Distributed checks

1. **Route prediction:** Multi-select every route that is physically shown.
2. **Term boundary:** Choose what a name can and cannot establish.
3. **Permanent-pool retrieval:** Flip-card questions distinguish normal water from temporary storm
   storage.
4. **Hydrograph reading:** Match peak, timing, and volume statements to the graph.
5. **Performance evidence:** Select which evidence supports a peak, volume, concentration, or load
   claim.
6. **Failure trace:** Separate observation, hypothesis, diagnosis, and authorized action.
7. **I&I classification:** Match direct inflow and defect-related infiltration examples without
   diagnosing a real system.
8. **Permit-value sequence:** Put responsibility, funded work, evidence, and correction in order.

### Final applied check

The learner receives an unfamiliar conceptual system with:

- one inflow;
- one visible storage area;
- one hidden route;
- one outlet;
- one overflow;
- one downstream condition;
- one maintenance observation;
- one possible wastewater connection;
- one permit or ownership clue; and
- incomplete evidence.

The learner completes the 11-question conversation tool. Passing requires every field, correct
separation of shown evidence from inference, a visible uncertainty statement, and no claim of
design adequacy, compliance, or site suitability.
